<div class="callout callout-finding">
<h4>This is a moving target — read the timeline first</h4>
As of this writing, LA is mid-collapse-and-rebuild of its core homelessness governance. <strong>April 2025</strong>: LA County voted to pull funding and staff out of LAHSA and build its own department. <strong>January 1, 2026</strong>: the new <strong>LA County Department of Homeless Services and Housing (HSH)</strong> launched. <strong>June 2026</strong>: HUD suspended LAHSA's federal funding over fraud allegations; LAHSA sued; a judge granted a stay pending an August 6, 2026 hearing. <strong>LAHSA's FY2026-27 budget</strong> dropped ~40%, from ~$829M to ~$496M, and it's now ~73% City-funded. As of this research date, it's genuinely unresolved whether LAHSA continues as a reduced joint agency, becomes a pure data/compliance shell, or is restructured again. Anything below should be checked against reporting dated after August 6, 2026.
</div>

## The core structural problem: a joint powers authority nobody fully owns

**LAHSA** (LA Homeless Services Authority) is a **joint powers authority**, created in 1993 specifically so LA City and County would stop fighting over who "owned" homelessness. It's governed by a 10-member Commission — 5 appointed by the County Board of Supervisors, 5 by the Mayor with Council confirmation.

<div class="callout callout-fails">
<h4>The JPA structure is a documented design flaw, not incidental</h4>
Because governance power is split evenly, neither government fully owns LAHSA — the County could vote to defund it, but the joint agreement still nominally gives it equal governing power, and the City can't unilaterally restructure or abolish it either. This structural ambiguity is cited repeatedly in coverage of the 2025-2026 breakup as the reason the split turned messy instead of clean.
</div>

LAHSA also serves as the **Collaborative Applicant** for the HUD-recognized LA Continuum of Care — meaning it's not just "a" homeless-services agency but the entity that legally applies for and administers nearly $1 billion in cumulative federal funding on behalf of the entire county's system. That dual role is why HUD's 2026 suspension threatens the whole county's system, not just LAHSA's own programs.

## The audit trail that triggered the breakup

A LA County Auditor-Controller review (finalized ~Feb. 2024) found LAHSA:

- Could not produce an accurate list of the agencies it contracts with, and made payments with no contract in place
- Had recovered only $2.5 million of $50.8 million in Measure H funds advanced in FY2017-18
- Failed to complete any of four planned internal audits in FY2022-23, and started only two of four planned in FY2023-24
- Had "almost no meaningful performance measures" in service contracts, and didn't enforce the few it had

HUD's June 2026 suspension cited a "clear pattern of fraud," failure to track empty motel/hotel rooms being billed to programs, a 2024 audit showing **$513 million in unspent funds**, and a $2.1 million conflict-of-interest contract tied to former CEO Va Lecia Adams Kellum's resignation (funds went to a nonprofit that employed her husband). The suspension put an estimated $150 million in already-awarded grants in limbo and blocked LAHSA from submitting the region's next ~$241 million CoC application.

## Where mandates overlap and people fall through gaps

1. **LAHSA vs. HSH vs. the CoC**: it's genuinely unclear who "owns" region-wide coordination right now. LAHSA retains the CoC's federal Collaborative Applicant role even as its operating budget and county staff have moved to HSH — reporters and advocates have flagged that no one has articulated what happens to CoC compliance if county capacity sits elsewhere while the "lead agency" designation doesn't.
2. **DPSS vs. LAHSA/CES**: family homeless assistance through CalWORKs is a parallel, means-tested system not obviously integrated with Coordinated Entry — a family that doesn't qualify for CalWORKs (certain immigration statuses, or single adults) has no equivalent DPSS pathway.
3. **Federal voucher supply vs. local outreach capacity**: Inside Safe can move people out of encampments into interim housing, but when HACLA's voucher pipeline pauses for budget reasons, there's no guaranteed permanent exit — a structural gap between the entities that find people and the entities that permanently house them.
4. **City Council district-by-district variation**: each of the 15 council districts runs its own "Homelessness Deputy" operation independently, meaning encampment response, sanitation, and service-linkage vary by which office covers a given block — de facto policy variation within one city.
5. **DMH/DPH vs. the housing system**: mental-health and public-health outreach teams have historically run their own caseloads largely in parallel with housing placement — HSH's stated FY2026-27 goals explicitly call for better coordinating them, an implicit admission they haven't been.

## Where the major agencies and NGOs don't reach — and who fills the gap

Every organization above is large enough to show up in an audit, a Form 990, or a City Council hearing. That scale is exactly why they cover the general-population pipeline this site maps on <a href="system.html">Phases of the Process</a> — and exactly why several real, documented populations and process steps fall outside what any of them actually do. This section names the specific organizations filling those gaps, at the same sourcing standard as the rest of this site, including their own limitations rather than treating "smaller" as a synonym for "better."

<div class="callout callout-finding">
<h4>The sharpest documented gap: domestic violence survivors are structurally invisible to the system that houses everyone else</h4>
Roughly <strong>4,000 people</strong> are homeless in the City of LA specifically because they fled domestic violence, and 44% of unhoused women surveyed county-wide in 2023 cited DV as the primary cause. The City has 17 DV shelters totaling <strong>567 beds</strong> — of nearly 16,000 people who called DV hotlines seeking shelter, only about <strong>10% got a bed</strong>. This isn't just a capacity gap: this site's <a href="tools.html">Tools &amp; Systems</a> page already establishes that Victim Service Providers are <em>legally barred</em> from entering client data into HMIS — meaning DV survivors served through the parallel, VOCA-funded DV shelter network (Jenesse Center, 1736 Family Crisis Center, Valley Oasis in the Antelope Valley, and The People Concern's own Sojourn program) are invisible to the same Coordinated Entry System that prioritizes everyone else. How — or whether — a DV survivor's need gets reflected in CES prioritization without an HMIS record was not documented in any source found; treat this as an open gap, not a confirmed integration. VOCA funding, the backbone of this entire parallel network, has faced steep, repeated cuts nationally. <a href="https://19thnews.org/2025/03/las-housing-crisis-is-trapping-domestic-violence-survivors/">The 19th</a> &middot; <a href="https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2023/12/domestic-violence-shelters/">CalMatters, on VOCA cuts</a>
</div>

**Legal aid / eviction defense** intervenes before someone enters this site's Phase 1 pipeline at all — at the point of an actual eviction filing — and none of the major shelter-operator nonprofits above do this work. **Legal Aid Foundation of LA (LAFLA)** leads **Stay Housed LA**, the city/county's coordinated eviction-defense infrastructure, alongside subcontractors **Bet Tzedek** and **Inner City Law Center**. In FY2023-24 the program fully represented roughly 2,800 tenants in court (52% stayed housed; 46% got a "soft landing") against LA County's ~45,000-47,000 annual eviction filings — meaning even a well-funded eviction-defense apparatus reaches on the order of 6% of county-wide filing volume. New Right to Counsel ordinances (LA County unincorporated areas and City of LA, both effective 2025) extend a legal *entitlement* to counsel for lower-income tenants, but whether funding and staffing will scale to match that entitlement is unresolved. ([PPIC](https://www.ppic.org/blog/evictions-in-california-have-leveled-off-with-upticks-in-some-counties/); [LAist, program figures](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/los-angeles-city-attorney-feldstein-soto-legal-aid-foundation-stay-housed-la-contract-audit))

<div class="callout callout-question">
<h4>The gap-fillers face their own version of LAHSA's accountability problems</h4>
LA's City Attorney launched an audit of LAFLA and, for more than a year, refused to authorize $177 million in already-approved Stay Housed LA funding, citing the nonprofit's alleged failure to account for how it spends city funds. LAFLA disputes this, saying it has provided detailed case-level data and invoicing throughout. This is a live, unresolved dispute, not a confirmed finding either way — but it belongs in the record for the same reason Urban Alchemy's contracting controversy does above: scrutiny of "who gets the contract and how it's monitored" is not unique to LAHSA. <a href="https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/los-angeles-city-attorney-feldstein-soto-legal-aid-foundation-stay-housed-la-contract-audit">LAist</a>
</div>

**Landlord engagement and master leasing** is the clearest case where the gap-filling function exists specifically because a documented coordination failure keeps recurring — see the master-leasing vacancy scandal and RFTA/lease-up bottleneck already mapped in the diagrams on <a href="system.html">Phases of the Process</a>. But the organizations doing this work are inconsistently placed in the system: sometimes a standalone nonprofit, sometimes a program bolted onto a major shelter operator, and at least once, absorbed directly into LAHSA itself. **Brilliant Corners** runs the county's Flexible Housing Subsidy Pool (FHSP) — see below for a dedicated profile. **PATH's LeaseUp** program, matching landlords to tenants since 2019, housed 1,000+ people through 600+ participating landlords by the time the program **transferred from PATH to LAHSA on July 1, 2024** — meaning LAHSA absorbed a specialized, apparently-functioning landlord-matching program into itself at almost exactly the same period its own separately-run master-leased portfolio was sitting at 34% vacancy. Whether that reflects a deliberate consolidation strategy or simply uneven internal capacity allocation is an open question this research couldn't resolve further. A related PATH program, Residential Property Support Services, had executed 123 property agreements across 3,181 units as of its most recent reporting. ([LAist, on PATH LeaseUp](https://laist.com/sponsored/landlords-path-leaseup-provides-stable-tenants-at-market-rates))

**Culturally- and population-specific providers** show a genuinely uneven pattern worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. Some populations have real, CES-integrated infrastructure: **Jovenes, Inc.** formally holds the CES Regional Coordinator role for transition-age youth (18-24) in SPA 7, and the **LA LGBT Center** — with roughly 117 dedicated youth housing beds/units across a transitional living program, crisis shelter, and PSH units — participates in the CES network alongside PATH and LA Family Housing. Others don't: this research found no Black-led organization that is both homelessness-exclusive and operating at a scale comparable to PATH or The People Concern, despite LA County's own Anti-Racism, Diversity, and Inclusion Initiative documenting the same Black/9.4%-of-population-vs-29-32%-of-unhoused-population disparity already on this site's <a href="overview.html">Scope &amp; Scale</a> page — and a 2026 CalMatters piece reports community-based, culturally-specific mental-health models for unhoused Black residents are currently *losing* funding. No comparable Latino-led, homelessness-exclusive direct-service organization at scale was found either, despite UCLA research and a county task force both independently documenting that language barriers, immigration-status fears, and institutional mistrust cause Latino residents to underutilize existing services and be undercounted. Nor was one found for LA's fastest-growing unhoused subgroup — adults 50+, who've grown from roughly 10% to roughly half the county's homeless population. ([LA County ARDI/BPEH](https://ceo.lacounty.gov/ardi/bpeh/); [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/commentary/2026/04/mental-health-black-california-homeless/); [UCLA LPPI](https://latino.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Stemming-the-Rise-of-Latino-Homelessness-2-1.pdf))

**Harm reduction** turns out to be a narrower gap than it first appears. Standalone organizations like **HHCLA Center for Harm Reduction** (Skid Row-concentrated syringe services, overdose prevention) and **Bienestar Human Services** still operate on separate public-health funding streams (HIV/Ryan White-adjacent, county Substance Abuse Prevention and Control money) rather than housing budgets — but LA County DHS has increasingly brought harm reduction in-house, running a formal Harm Reduction Division that served roughly 3,000 unique unsheltered patients in 2024 through the same Multidisciplinary Teams already documented on <a href="system.html">Phases of the Process</a>. Whether the standalone NGOs' contacts feed into CES/HMIS in any formal way was not confirmed in any source found — an open gap, not a confirmed disconnection.

**Geography** matters too: the Antelope Valley (SPA 1) — more than half of LA County's land area — has historically had fewer shelters, mental-health facilities, and PSH units than the rest of the county, with **Valley Oasis** functioning as the region's primary combined DV/homelessness provider out of a single 65-bed shelter. The region saw a 37% drop in unsheltered homelessness in the most recent reporting period, credited to HSH's Pathway Home program and Homekey investment — real progress, off a historically thin base.

<div class="callout callout-question">
<h4>Reading this section through the analytical frame</h4>
Some of these gaps are population-level (no org at scale for Black or Latino or older unhoused residents specifically) and some are structural (DV survivors excluded from HMIS by law; landlord-engagement functions bouncing between a nonprofit and LAHSA itself). Per <a href="sopk.html">The Analytical Frame</a>, the population-level gaps are partly a psychology/trust finding — the same institutional-mistrust dynamic documented in the VI-SPDAT racial-bias finding on <a href="tools.html">Tools &amp; Systems</a> plausibly explains why some communities haven't produced (or haven't been resourced to build) a homelessness-specific organization at PATH's scale, even where the underlying need is well documented. The structural gaps are a system-appreciation finding: nobody designed VOCA funding and HUD CoC funding to talk to each other, so DV survivors fall through a seam nobody owns, not because anyone decided they should.
</div>

## Brilliant Corners: a peer-level profile

This organization didn't make the original nonprofit table above — a gap worth correcting, since it operates at exactly the CES-match-to-lease-up handoff this site has already documented as one of the system's clearest breaks. It's profiled here at the same standard applied to every other organization on this site: real scale, real evidence, and real scrutiny, including where the evidence is thin or unflattering.

**What it actually does.** Brilliant Corners was founded in 2004 to house people with intellectual and developmental disabilities transitioning out of institutional settings, and expanded into general homelessness work in 2014 when LA County selected it to run the newly created **Flexible Housing Subsidy Pool (FHSP)**. It is not a street-outreach or shelter operator — it does no outreach and only light tenancy support (intensive case management for FHSP participants is contracted separately to 75 community-based organizations). Its actual functions: fiscal intermediary for the subsidy dollars, a dedicated 12-person landlord-facing housing-acquisitions team ("like a kind of real estate agency for the unhoused," per NPR), master lessor on specific interim-housing sites, and — through a separate role (CIIP) — a capital-projects intermediary managing construction funding for new interim-housing beds. ([Brilliant Corners](https://brilliantcorners.org/about/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/10/24/1207461308/homelessness-housing-losangeles-la-real-estate-unhoused))

**Scale.** LA County describes the Flex Pool's budget as "over $200 million" without clarifying whether that's annual or cumulative — a real ambiguity, not a rounding choice. Program-to-date, one federal report puts cumulative FHSP funding at over $350 million since 2014, moving roughly $10 million/month in rent subsidies and housing 12,000-16,000+ people since inception at roughly 190-200 people/month. Organization-wide (not LA-specific — Brilliant Corners also operates in San Francisco, San Mateo, the Inland Empire, and San Diego), FY2024 revenue was reported at approximately $116 million, though prior-year filings show substantial swings (as high as ~$415M in a year that likely recognized a large capital grant) that this research could not independently confirm the cause of. ([LA County HSH](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/innovations-in-housing/); [ACL.gov, HSRC braided-funding report](https://acl.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Housing-for-Health-Braided-Funding-Model-California-2025-HSRC.pdf))

<h4>92% one-year retention is real, but it's a self-reported figure repeated across sources, not three independent measurements</h4>
LA County's own materials, Brilliant Corners' own materials, and a third-party foundation-award profile all cite the same 92% figure — it traces to one underlying claim, not independent replication. The closest thing to an outside evaluation found is California Policy Lab research on Time-Limited Subsidy programs broadly (a related but not identical model), which found a 25% reduction in subsequent homeless-services enrollment among 3,677 tracked adults — real, methodologically transparent, but not a direct evaluation of Brilliant Corners' own administration. No LA-specific "cost per successful exit" figure exists for this program either, consistent with this site's broader finding that the metric is largely absent system-wide. <a href="https://capolicylab.org/news/new-research-finds-time-limited-housing-subsidy-programs-can-help-reduce-homelessness-in-los-angeles/">California Policy Lab</a>

<div class="callout callout-mixed">
<h4>Real, documented workforce and legal findings — not manufactured for balance</h4>
A union election was certified at Brilliant Corners' Los Angeles operations in March 2024: NLRB Case 21-RC-335075, 314 eligible voters, 239 ballots counted, 178-61 in favor of representation by OPEIU Local 30. Brilliant Corners' own theory of change — that the bottleneck between a subsidy and a lease is the landlord-facing transaction, not the subsidy dollars themselves — is directly consistent with what this site has independently documented elsewhere: the paperwork gauntlet on <a href="system.html">Phases of the Process</a>, HACLA's own admitted RFTA delays and understaffing, and the named Yolanda Robins and Halcyon Selfmade cases where the failure was specifically landlord/paperwork friction, not a lack of subsidy. That makes the model theory-consistent with this site's own independent findings — but the LA-specific proof that it executes better than the default system remains, on the evidence found here, mostly self-reported rather than independently audited.
