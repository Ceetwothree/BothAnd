### The 2026 count in detail (most recent — released July 24, 2026)

- Countywide: **73,040** people experiencing homelessness, up **1.2%** from the 2025 baseline of 72,195. City of LA: **45,194**, up **3.4%** from 43,695. LAHSA characterized both increases as not statistically significant. [FOX 11, "LA homelessness rises in 2026 count as economic strain, subsidy cuts stall progress"](https://www.foxla.com/news/los-angeles-homeless-count-2026-increase-lahsa-report); [LAist, "LA homelessness rose in 2026, official count shows"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homelessness-rose-in-2026-official-count-shows).
- This ends two consecutive years of modest declines (2024, 2025) — the county total remains below the 2023 (75,518) and 2024 (75,312) peaks, but the direction reversed. [Santa Monica Mirror, 2026 coverage](https://smmirror.com/2026-homeless-count-unhoused-population-rises-across-l-a-city-and-l-a-county/).
- Drivers cited by LAHSA: an influx of people living in RVs, and a **funding reduction in Time-Limited Subsidies** that left roughly 5,000 fewer people receiving permanent housing placements. The number of people moving from the street into interim housing fell 8%, and the number transitioning from interim into permanent housing fell 36%. [LAist, "LA's homeless count results are out tomorrow..."](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homeless-count-results-2026-bass); [FOX 11, 2026 coverage](https://www.foxla.com/news/los-angeles-homeless-count-2026-increase-lahsa-report).
- City unsheltered population grew to roughly 29,000; sheltered population declined to roughly 16,000 (a 3.9% drop, down 644 people) even as unsheltered grew 7.9% (up 2,143 people). [LAist, 2026 coverage](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homeless-count-results-2026-bass).
- Not all sub-regions rose: homelessness fell 29.6% in the Antelope Valley, 12% in the South Bay, 10.2% in West LA, and 4.3% in the San Fernando Valley in 2026 — the citywide/countywide increase was concentrated elsewhere. [WestsideToday, 2026 coverage](https://westsidetoday.com/2026/07/25/2026-homeless-count-unhoused-population-rises-across-l-a-city-and-l-a-county/).
- Family homelessness in the LA Continuum of Care fell 14%, from 11,207 (2025) to 9,638 (2026) — see caveats about how family counts are measured, below. [WestsideToday, 2026 coverage](https://westsidetoday.com/2026/07/25/2026-homeless-count-unhoused-population-rises-across-l-a-city-and-l-a-county/).
- Veteran homelessness continued falling even as the overall count rose: down from roughly 3,050 to 2,257 (a drop of 793 people). [LAist, 2026 coverage](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homelessness-rose-in-2026-official-count-shows).

### PIT count methodology — how it actually works

- HUD requires a biennial unsheltered PIT count as a condition of Continuum of Care (CoC) funding; LAHSA has conducted it **annually** (rather than the HUD-minimum every-other-year) since 2016, specifically to track trends more granularly. [Reconstructed from search-aggregated LAHSA methodology descriptions — verify against LAHSA's own methodology PDF.]
- The count has three main data components: (1) a physical **unsheltered tally** — volunteers in teams of ~4, covering assigned census tracts across roughly 4,000 square miles of LA County over one to three nights in late January, counting people, tents, vehicles, and makeshift shelters they observe (no interviews are conducted during the tally itself); (2) a separate **demographic survey** of a sample of unsheltered adults and a **youth count/survey**, used to extrapolate demographic percentages onto the raw tally; and (3) **administrative data from HMIS** (the Homeless Management Information System) for the sheltered population, which is counted by name/record rather than by street tally. [USC Dworak-Peck, 2024 Homeless Count Methodology Report — cited via search]; [Home for Good / LA County description of PIT components].
- As of the 2023 count, volunteers recorded tallies via a mobile app built with Esri, with paper maps/tally sheets as a backup for technical failures. [Search-aggregated LAHSA/County description of 2023 count logistics.]
- The 2025 count itself was **postponed from its normal January date to February 18–20, 2025** because of the deadly LA County wildfires that January. [LAist, "Why hasn't LA released results of this year's homeless count?"](https://laist.com/news/health/homeless-count-delay-lahsa-hud-data-release-results-review-lawsuit) (title/topic confirmed via search; full article text could not be fetched directly — see methodology note).

### Documented criticism and limitations of the PIT/count process

This is one of the most important and best-documented findings of this research pass:

1. **RAND: the official count is missing a large and possibly growing share of unsheltered people in specific LA neighborhoods.** A RAND Corporation study (RAND's "LA LEADS" research report, released via RAND's report series in fall 2025) found that the official PIT tally captured only about **68% of the unsheltered population** across three key City of LA neighborhoods studied (Hollywood, Skid Row, and Venice), when cross-checked against a more intensive, professionally-conducted independent count. Breaking that down further: Hollywood's official PIT total was about 81% of RAND's independent estimate, Venice fell in between, and **Skid Row's official count captured only about 61%** of RAND's estimate — meaning the area most associated with visible homelessness in LA may be undercounted by nearly 40%. [RAND, "Growing Inaccuracies in Official Counts Jeopardize LA Homelessness Wins"](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4438-1.html); [LAist, "LA's official homeless tally increasingly undercounts people on the streets, RAND study finds"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/lahsa-homeless-count-rand-accuracy-2025-volunteers-report-study).
   - RAND's proposed mechanism: as city initiatives like Inside Safe cleared the largest, most visible encampments (the easiest subpopulation to count accurately), the unsheltered population that remains has become harder to locate and tally — meaning the same volunteer-based methodology that worked reasonably well a few years ago is now systematically less accurate, potentially making genuine progress look larger than it is (or masking backsliding).
   - RAND's recommendation: rely more heavily on **professional field teams to independently cross-check volunteer counts**, calling this solution relatively inexpensive and potentially fundable through Measure A revenue.
   - **Numbers to flag as inconsistent/uncertain across sources:** search summaries of this same RAND report described its headline finding two different ways — "captured just 68% of the unsheltered population" versus "undercounted... by 32%" (which is roughly the same claim stated as a miss-rate rather than a capture-rate, i.e. 100% − 68% ≈ 32%) and a separate, larger claim that "homelessness [was] up 18% in LA hot spots where the official count recorded decreases." These appear to be consistent restatements of the same underlying study rather than contradictory studies, but this should be verified against RAND's full PDF before quoting precise percentages in published copy — the current research pass could not open the RAND report directly due to a tooling access issue (see final Sources note).
2. **Broader academic/advocacy literature on PIT undercounting** (national, not LA-specific): estimates in the literature suggest the true scale of homelessness nationally could be **2.5 to 10.2 times** what a single-night PIT count captures, largely because PIT methodology structurally excludes people who are couch-surfing/doubled-up, undercounts families and youth (HUD's sheltered-count methodology is widely criticized for missing school-identified homeless children — the U.S. Department of Education counted **more than 1.4 million** homeless students in 2022–2023, vastly more than what any PIT count reflects, because McKinney-Vento's broader "homeless" definition for schools includes doubled-up families that HUD's PIT count does not count as homeless at all), and because rough sleepers often have direct incentives (safety, shame, fear of enforcement/child-welfare involvement) to stay hidden from counters. [SchoolHouse Connection, "The Pitfalls of HUD's Point-in-Time Count for Children, Youth, and Families"](https://schoolhouseconnection.org/article/the-pitfalls-of-huds-point-in-time-count-for-children-youth-and-families); [ABC News, "'You can't be accurate': Annual count of US homeless population misses large numbers of people, experts warn"](https://abcnews.com/US/accurate-annual-count-us-homeless-population-misses-large/story?id=106671876).
3. **Weather dependency and single-night snapshot problems**: because the count happens on one specific (often cold/wet) January night, results can be skewed by short-term shelter-seeking behavior (e.g., people who normally sleep outside taking a motel room that week due to timing of a benefits check, or conversely staying more visible/outside than usual). [Wikipedia, "Point-In-Time Count"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-In-Time_Count) — general description, consistent with search-aggregated criticism above.
4. **Methodological inconsistency across jurisdictions and years**: HUD sets few standards beyond requiring a single date; the actual canvassing method, volunteer training, and quality-assurance process vary community to community and can change year to year within the same community (LA's own methodology has shifted — e.g., new mobile-app tooling in 2023, added quality-assurance backups) — which makes clean multi-year trend comparisons harder than the headline percent-change figures suggest.
5. **A separate, LA-specific 2024 controversy**: LAist reported that a "complicated 2024 homeless count" led some city officials and experts to question the count's accuracy that year specifically — worth a follow-up read before publishing 2024 comparisons as fully reliable. [LAist, "Did a complicated 2024 homeless count lead to errors? Experts and some city officials think so"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/los-angeles-homeless-count-2024-accuracy-questions) (title/topic found via search; full text not independently verified in this pass).
6. **By-name lists as an alternative/complementary approach**: some advocates and researchers (e.g., Community Solutions' "Built for Zero" framework) argue that a continuously updated, real-time "by-name list" of every known homeless individual — updated at least monthly via outreach and HMIS records, rather than a single-night volunteer tally — is a more accurate and actionable measurement approach than the annual PIT count, though LA's system still relies primarily on the PIT count for official reporting. [Community Solutions, "Yes, there's a better way to measure homelessness than the annual point-in-time count"](https://community.solutions/yes-theres-a-better-way-to-measure-homelessness-than-the-annual-point-in-time-count/).

### Chronic, episodic, and first-time homelessness

- **HUD's definition of chronic homelessness**: a person with a disabling condition who has been continuously homeless (in a place not meant for human habitation, a safe haven, or emergency shelter) for at least 12 months, OR who has had at least four separate homeless episodes in the last three years that together total at least 12 months. [HUD Exchange, "Definition of Chronic Homelessness"](https://www.hudexchange.info/homelessness-assistance/coc-esg-homeless-eligibility/definition-of-chronic-homelessness/).
- **Episodic homelessness** is generally used to describe people cycling in and out of homelessness repeatedly (commonly defined as three or more distinct episodes in the past year) without yet meeting the full 12-month chronic threshold.
- **Nationally**, roughly 10–15% of people who ever experience homelessness go on to become chronically homeless, though on any given night chronically homeless individuals are a much larger share of the *point-in-time* unsheltered population (because by definition they stay homeless far longer, they are disproportionately likely to be present on any single night's tally — a form of "length-biased sampling" worth noting for the site). [Search-aggregated from USICH / HUD chronic-homelessness literature — no LA-specific percentage of "all who ever experience homelessness" was found in this pass.]
- **LA-specific chronic homelessness trend**: chronic homelessness fell 6.8% (LA County) in the 2024 count versus 2023, split between a 9.4% drop among unsheltered chronically homeless people and a 7.5% *increase* among sheltered chronically homeless people (i.e., more chronically homeless people were successfully in shelter, not necessarily fewer overall). In the 2025 count, the *sheltered* chronic homelessness population reportedly increased by 162 people. [LAHSA 2024 results coverage — search-aggregated]; [search-aggregated 2025 chronic figure — could not independently verify against LAHSA's own table; flagged as needing confirmation].
- **This research could not find a reliable, current LA-specific breakdown clearly separating "first-time" homelessness from returning/repeat homelessness** as a headline statistic in the 2024–2026 counts (a commonly cited "44% first-time" figure could not be confirmed in this pass and should not be used without locating it in LAHSA's own demographic survey report).

### Comparison to other major U.S. metros

- Per HUD's **2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report (AHAR)** — covering January 2025 PIT counts nationally, published to Congress in 2026 — the national total was **745,652** people experiencing homelessness on a single night, a 3.4% decrease from 2024 and the first year-over-year national decrease since 2016. [NLIHC, "HUD 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report Finds First Reduction in Overall Homelessness in Nearly a Decade"](https://nlihc.org/resource/hud-2025-annual-homelessness-assessment-report-finds-first-reduction-overall-homelessness); [HUD, "HUD Releases 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report to Congress"](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037); [HUD USER, 2025 AHAR Part 1 PDF](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/2025-AHAR-Part-1.pdf).
- By raw CoC-level population, the **New York City CoC (125,683)** and the **Los Angeles CoC (67,777, per the 2025 AHAR's use of the finalized/HUD-reviewed figure)** were the two largest homeless populations of any CoC nationally in the 2025 AHAR. Seattle/King County CoC came in with roughly **16,936** — notably smaller in absolute terms, though Seattle's homelessness is disproportionately unsheltered (over 57% of Seattle's homeless population is unsheltered, a similar unsheltered-heavy pattern to LA and San Francisco). [Search-aggregated AHAR-based reporting.]
- A key structural distinction worth highlighting for readers: **New York City and Boston have large *sheltered* populations** — NYC in particular operates under a legally enforceable "right to shelter" — while **San Francisco, Portland, Seattle, Long Beach, and Los Angeles combine severe housing-cost pressure with substantial *unsheltered* homelessness**, meaning LA's crisis is visually and functionally different from NYC's even when the total headcounts are in a similar range: LA's homelessness is much more visible on the street, NYC's is much more contained within a large shelter system. This is one of the more useful framing points for the site to make explicit.
- One cited per-capita figure — **NYC at 165.3 per 10,000 residents, San Francisco at 100.6 per 10,000** — was found via search but could not be independently verified against a primary HUD/Census source in this pass and should be re-derived directly from AHAR + Census population data before publishing exact per-capita rates for LA, NYC, SF, and Seattle side by side.
- **This research recommends a dedicated follow-up pass reading the full 2025 AHAR PDF directly** (huduser.gov) to build an accurate, citable comparison table (total count, unsheltered share, per-capita rate) for LA vs. NYC vs. SF vs. Seattle — the tool access issues in this session prevented reading that PDF directly.

### Recent controversy in how the count/system itself is conducted and reported

This turned out to be a major and highly current storyline as of mid-2026, worth prominent treatment on the site:

- **HUD review and revision of the 2025 count**: after LAHSA released preliminary 2025 results, a HUD review found duplication and utilization errors (two Long Beach-area interim housing sites had been mistakenly folded into the LA CoC count; eight sites were removed at HUD's request for irregular utilization rates). LAHSA held finalized results pending HUD sign-off; then-interim CEO Gita O'Neill said the agency would wait "to ensure total data integrity." The net revision was small (LA CoC total revised down by 141 people; countywide unsheltered estimate revised up by 37) and LAHSA characterized all revisions as within the margin of error. [LAHSA, "Releases Finalized 2025 Homeless Count Results After HUD Review"](https://www.lahsa.org/news?article=1051-lahsa-releases-finalized-2025-homeless-count-results-after-hud-review); [WestsideToday, "LAHSA Finalizes 2025 Homeless Count After Federal Review, Confirms Minimal Changes Across Los Angeles"](https://westsidetoday.com/2025/10/10/lahsa-finalizes-2025-homeless-count-after-federal-review-confirms-minimal-changes-across-los-angeles/).
- **LAHSA leadership turnover**: CEO Va Lecia Adams Kellum resigned after a roughly two-year tenure; Gita O'Neill (an assistant LA city attorney and former director of homeless policy/strategy for the City Attorney's office) was named interim CEO in mid-2025, tasked with stabilizing the agency amid the transition of county functions to a new county department (see below). [LAist, "Gita O'Neill named as new LAHSA head with major changes on the horizon"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/oneill-new-lahsa-head); [LAist, "LAHSA leader resigns"](https://laist.com/brief/news/housing-homelessness/los-angeles-homeless-services-authority-ceo-va-lecia-adams-kellum-resigns-lahsa).
- **Financial-management audit findings**: an audit covering the fiscal year ending June 2025 found poor bookkeeping and accounting of over $800 million in public funds; LAHSA reportedly underspent its overall budget by $108 million that fiscal year due to "program delays," and a separate court-ordered audit found the agency's records made it "impossible to accurately track spending or the performance outcomes of its vendors," including dollars sent to nonprofit providers without formal agreements governing repayment terms. [Search-aggregated audit reporting — recommend locating the underlying audit document itself (likely LA City Controller or a court-ordered auditor) for direct citation before publication.]
- **HUD suspended federal funding to LAHSA in June 2026** amid a fraud investigation, citing "a clear pattern of fraud," failure to track empty hotel rooms under contract, a 2024 audit showing **$513 million in unspent funds**, a $2.1 million conflict-of-interest contract, and LAHSA's 2025 inability to provide verification paperwork showing the locations of roughly 2,300 housing sites. LAHSA has received nearly $1 billion from the federal government since 2021 and had until roughly July 11, 2026 to request a hearing contesting the suspension. [Yahoo/AP wire, "HUD suspends federal funding to LA homelessness agency LAHSA amid fraud probe"](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/hud-suspends-federal-homeless-funding-171554996.html); [FOX 11, same story](https://www.foxla.com/news/hud-suspends-los-angeles-lahsa-homeless-funding-fraud); [NBC LA, "Federal funding for LAHSA suspended over 'failures'"](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/lahsa-homeless-funding-hud/3903074/). **This is a very recent, fast-moving, and consequential story that this site should track closely and update as it resolves** — as of this research date (2026-08-01) the outcome of any hearing was not yet found.
- **LA County is building its own homeless-services department, separate from LAHSA**: on July 1, 2025 the LA County Board of Supervisors approved creating the **LA County Department of Homeless Services and Housing**, consolidating homelessness-related work currently spread across roughly 14 county departments and bringing it under direct Board of Supervisors oversight, effectively withdrawing county funding from LAHSA and redeploying it to a new direct-service county department. The new department launched January 1, 2026, with a full transition away from LAHSA-run county programs targeted for July 1, 2026; it is expected to handle roughly $1 billion in funding and employ around 1,000 people. **LAHSA is not being dissolved** — it continues as the lead agency for the Los Angeles Continuum of Care, still manages HMIS (the region's shared homeless-services data system), and still leads the Point-in-Time count itself, but it is losing its county-service-delivery role and a large share of its historic funding/scope. [SMDP, "County votes to break with longtime homelessness agency and form new department"](https://www.smdp.com/county-votes-to-break-with-longtime-homelessness-agency-and-form-new-department/); [LA County CEO, "Department on Homelessness"](https://ceo.lacounty.gov/dh/); [Supervisor Lindsey Horvath, "LA County Takes Action for a More Accountable Homeless Services System"](https://lindseyhorvath.lacounty.gov/new-homeless-department/); [NBC LA, "LA County approves over $840M for its own homelessness agency"](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/los-angeles-county-department-of-homeless-services-and-housing-funding/3840891/). **This is a major structural change that should probably get its own dedicated treatment on the "agencies" page of the site**, since it changes who is actually responsible for county-funded services going forward.
- **LA Alliance v. City of Los Angeles litigation continues to shape both the count and the broader system**: as of a June 2025 ruling, Judge David O. Carter found the City in breach of key 2022 settlement terms — including failing to deliver a plan for 12,915 beds by 2027, missing housing milestones, and "flouting" its duty to report accurate data — but declined the LA Alliance's request for full receivership of the City's homelessness system, instead ordering the appointment of an independent third-party monitor (Daniel Gerrie and Ron Galperin were the proposed monitors, pending City Council approval) with "unfettered access" to City and LAHSA data systems, plus quarterly hearings before the court. The court has said it will retain jurisdiction until June 30, 2027. Settlement terms also required the City to fund 3,000 new mental health/substance-use-disorder beds and 450 new Adult Residential Facility/RCFE subsidies by 2026. [The Planning Report, "LA Alliance for Human Rights v City of LA: Judge Carter Rules Against Receivership"](https://www.planningreport.com/2025/07/03/la-alliance-human-rights-v-city-la-judge-carter-rules-against-receivership); [CityWatch LA, "Judge Carter Rejects Receivership, Orders Data Monitor: 5 Takeaways on L.A.'s Homelessness Failures"](https://www.citywatchla.com/los-angeles/31070-judge-carter-rejects-receivership-orders-data-monitor-5-takeaways-on-l-a-s-homelessness-failures); [Civil Rights Litigation Clearinghouse case page](https://clearinghouse.net/case/43959/); [CD4/Nithya Raman, "An Update on the LA Alliance Case"](https://cd4.lacity.gov/newsletter/an-update-on-the-la-alliance-case/).

## Phases of the Process

*This section maps the intended end-to-end pipeline a person experiencing (or at risk of) homelessness moves through in LA. Agency detail is intentionally kept light per the research brief — a separate research pass covers agency profiles in depth — and the CES/VI-SPDAT tool is described only at a high level, since a separate pass covers that tool specifically.*

### 1. Prevention / diversion

- **Prevention** targets people at *imminent* risk of homelessness — LAHSA's Homeless Prevention Program is aimed at people who have, e.g., been given a 30-day notice to vacate — and provides short-term (generally up to six months) financial assistance and housing-stabilization case management intended to help someone keep their current housing or find alternative housing before they become literally homeless. Eligibility is means-tested and prioritized using a **Prevention Targeting Tool**: households scoring above a defined risk-score cutoff receive full prevention assistance (financial aid + case management); households below the cutoff receive a lighter-touch service instead of full assistance — meaning many at-risk households are screened out of intensive help by design. Some subsidized-housing participants can qualify with income up to 80% of Area Median Income (AMI) if they've also received prior homeless housing assistance. [Search-aggregated from LAHSA/LA County prevention program descriptions](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/prevention/); a formal evaluation exists: [California Policy Lab, "Evaluation of Los Angeles County Measure H-Funded Homelessness [Prevention]"](https://www.capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Prevention-Evaluation-full-report.pdf) (evaluates Measure H-funded prevention specifically — a useful primary source for a deeper future pass on effectiveness).
- **Diversion** is conceptually distinct from prevention: it targets people who are already homeless or about to enter the shelter/CES system *right now*, using a "problem-solving" conversation-based approach — case managers help the person identify any viable option within their own network (family, friends) or existing resources to avoid ever entering the formal homeless system, sometimes backed by small, one-time flexible financial assistance (e.g., a bus ticket, a small rent gap payment) as a last resort. [Search-aggregated from LA County Homeless Initiative program descriptions.]
- Agencies involved: LAHSA administers much of the funded prevention/diversion contracting; LA County's Department of Public Social Services, DCFS, and (going forward) the new County Department of Homeless Services and Housing also fund prevention-adjacent programs. Scale (dollar amount, number of households served annually) could not be confirmed with a specific current-year figure in this research pass — **flagged as a gap** for a follow-up pass, ideally reading LAHSA's or the County's own budget/performance documents directly.

### 2. Street outreach

- Multiple outreach systems operate in parallel across the City and County, with different clinical focuses:
  - **LA County Department of Mental Health (DMH) HOME Teams** ("Homeless Outreach & Mobile Engagement"): specialized outreach and intensive case management for unhoused adults with serious mental illness, including people who have declined other services — described as using a "relentless engagement" model with "live handoffs" to connect people directly and continuously to treatment and housing rather than one-off referrals. HOME Teams operate in every County Service Planning Area. [LA County DMH, HOME Program description](https://dmh.lacounty.gov/our-services/countywide-services/home/).
  - **LA County Department of Health Services (DHS), Housing for Health Division — Multidisciplinary Teams (MDTs)**: outreach staffed with a mix of physical health, mental health, substance use, case management, and peer-support expertise, generally serving clients with more complex/co-occurring health and behavioral health needs than a single-discipline team can address. [Search-aggregated from LA County DHS outreach program descriptions.]
  - **City of LA "Inside Safe"** (Mayor Karen Bass's signature initiative, launched 2022): a voluntary, location-based strategy — the Mayor's Field Intervention Team (FIT), a multidisciplinary team including people with lived experience of homelessness, engages a specific targeted encampment location (assessed and selected in advance), working alongside other outreach providers (e.g., USC Street Medicine) to build a holistic picture of who is at that site before offering voluntary placement into interim housing, followed by case management, mental health care, income assistance, and transition planning. [Mayor Karen Bass, "Inside Safe" program page](https://mayor.lacity.gov/InsideSafe).
  - **City of LA "CIRCLE"** ("Crisis and Incident Response through Community-Led Engagement"): a 24/7 unarmed crisis-response program deploying mental-health professionals and people with lived experience to respond to non-violent LAPD calls involving unhoused individuals in crisis, as an alternative to a police-only response; as of recent reporting it operates in Hollywood, Downtown LA, South LA, Venice, and parts of the San Fernando Valley, and has been expanding into additional neighborhoods (e.g., Sherman Oaks, West LA) through 2025–2026. Access is via LAPD's non-emergency line, (877) 275-5273. [Mayor Karen Bass, CIRCLE expansion announcements](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/proven-crisis-and-incident-response-through-community-led-engagement-safety-program-expands).
  - **Public/general referral pathway**: members of the public can request outreach to a specific unhoused individual via the **LA-HOP** portal (www.LA-HOP.org), which triages requests to the appropriate outreach team (e.g., DMH HOME when a serious mental illness indicator is present). [Search-aggregated from LA County DMH outreach description.]
- No single reliable current figure for total outreach-team headcount, response-time, or annual-contacts-made was found in this pass across all these teams combined — this is a plausible area for a future research/data pass if the site wants to quantify outreach capacity relative to the unsheltered population size.

### 3. Coordinated Entry System (CES) — high-level only

- CES is LA County's single coordinated intake/prioritization framework, in place since roughly 2013, meant to replace an earlier first-come-first-served, provider-by-provider approach (where housing vacancies were filled inconsistently and the most vulnerable people could be stuck on separate, disconnected waitlists indefinitely) with one shared system: a person is assessed once, their information and vulnerability score are logged into **HMIS**, and permanent housing and other resources are then matched centrally according to a shared **Prioritization and Matching Policy**, rather than each provider running its own intake. [LAHSA, "Coordinated Entry System - CES Defined"](https://www.lahsa.org/ces/home/about/); [LAHSA Document Library, "Prioritization And Matching Policy For The Los Angeles Coordinated Entry System"](https://www.lahsa.org/documents?id=6273-prioritization-and-matching-policy-for-the-los-angeles-coordinated-entry-system.pdf).
- Historically, LA's CES used the **VI-SPDAT** ("Vulnerability Index-Service Prioritization Decision Assistance Tool") — an oral, points-based survey administered by a case manager, where a higher "acuity score" is meant to correspond to greater vulnerability and therefore higher priority for permanent supportive housing and other intensive resources. *(Per the research brief, a separate pass covers the VI-SPDAT tool itself in depth — this note is intentionally high-level.)* [Search-aggregated; also see: The Markup, "How We Investigated L.A.'s Homelessness Scoring System"](https://themarkup.org/show-your-work/2023/02/28/how-we-investigated-l-a-s-homelessness-scoring-system) — an investigative deep-dive specifically on this scoring system, useful as the primary source for the dedicated VI-SPDAT research pass.
- **Documented criticism of CES**: Mayor Karen Bass has publicly criticized the system directly, saying "the current coordinated entry system is dysfunctional, impractical and inequitable," and noting housing units can sit vacant "for far too long" while the matching process runs. Separately, California Policy Lab research (see aftercare section below) found the CES/PSH matching process itself produces **racially disparate outcomes**, with Black unhoused residents specifically underprioritized relative to need in the matching process. In 2023 the LA City Council moved to direct reform of CES specifically to "house Angelenos sooner." [MyNewsLA, "City Council to Reform its Coordinated Entry System, House Angelenos Sooner"](https://mynewsla.com/business/2023/03/24/city-council-to-reform-its-coordinated-entry-system-house-angelenos-sooner/); [Knock LA, "Inside LA's Endless Queues for Housing, Care, and Survival"](https://knock-la.com/inside-la-endless-queues-for-housing/).
- No reliable current-year figure for typical CES-to-housing wait time was found in this pass (a commonly-cited but unverified claim is that placements can take a year or more) — flagged as a gap for the dedicated CES/VI-SPDAT research pass to fill with a primary source.

### 4. Interim / crisis housing

- The category includes **emergency shelters**, **bridge housing** (the City's "A Bridge Home" program), **tiny home villages**, and **motel/hotel voucher programs**. These are explicitly meant to function as a *temporary bridge* to permanent housing, not an end state.
- **A Bridge Home**: launched under then-Mayor Garcetti; the first site (El Puente, near El Pueblo) opened September 2018 with 45 beds using a "sleeping module" congregate model (cubicle-style rooms with shared hygiene facilities). The program has since incorporated tiny-home-village formats — e.g., Chandler Boulevard Bridge Home Village (40 units / 75 beds), Hope on Hart Street (50 units), Tarzana Tiny Home Village (76 units / 150 beds), Whitsett West Tiny Home Village (77 units / 150 beds). As of 2022, the City's Bureau of Engineering had delivered 12 tiny-home villages totaling roughly **1,552 interim beds** — this is a dated figure (2022) and the current total is almost certainly higher given continued construction through 2025–2026; a current total was not found in this pass. [HUD USER case study, "Chandler Boulevard Tiny Homes Village"](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/casestudies/study-092823.html); [Hope the Mission, "Tiny Homes Villages"](https://hopethemission.org/our-programs/tiny-homes-villages/).
- **Documented critique — the "bridge to nowhere" problem**: a LAist investigation ("A Bridge To Where?") found that relatively few people placed in LA's bridge/interim shelters actually exit into permanent housing, because permanent housing supply (deeply affordable units and rental subsidies) is far more scarce than interim shelter capacity — meaning every case manager and every resident is effectively competing for a small number of permanent slots downstream. One expert quoted in that reporting warned against investing heavily in emergency/bridge housing *without* a credible permanent-housing exit strategy, since doing so risks "warehousing people indefinitely" rather than actually bridging them anywhere. [LAist, "A Bridge To Where? Few Exit LA's 'Temporary' Homeless Shelters For Permanent Housing"](https://laist.com/news/bridge-home-los-angeles-garcetti-result).
- This dynamic is corroborated by the 2026 PIT count finding cited above: the number of people transitioning from interim into *permanent* housing fell 36% year-over-year, even as the system kept moving people from the street into interim beds — i.e., the front door of interim housing was still functioning while the back door (permanent placement) was narrowing, which is close to the exact failure mode the LAist reporting warned about. [LAist, 2026 count results coverage](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homeless-count-results-2026-bass).
- Total current interim/emergency bed inventory for the LA CoC is tracked in LAHSA's annual **Housing Inventory Count (HIC)**, which is published alongside the PIT count, but this research pass could not access LAHSA's HIC dashboard or tables directly (see methodology note) — **recommended as a priority item for a follow-up pass**, since a current total-beds-vs-total-unsheltered-population comparison would be one of the single most useful numbers for this page.
- Agencies/operators: the City's Bureau of Engineering designs/builds tiny-home sites; a mix of nonprofit operators run day-to-day site operations (e.g., Hope the Mission runs multiple tiny-home villages); LAHSA and LA County (increasingly, the new Department of Homeless Services and Housing) fund and coordinate placement into these sites via CES.

### 5. Permanent housing placement: Rapid Re-Housing (RRH) vs. Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH)

- **Rapid Re-Housing (RRH)**: short-to-medium-term rental subsidy (HUD guidance describes short-term assistance up to about 3 months and medium-term assistance from about 4–24 months) paired with housing-search and relocation services, intended to move people into permanent housing as quickly as possible and then taper off support as the household stabilizes. HUD does **not** require a disability to qualify for RRH — it's generally the lower-barrier, faster-turnaround track, aimed at households who need help specifically with the transition/search process and a temporary financial bridge rather than indefinite subsidy. [HUD Exchange, "CoC Program Components - Rapid Re-housing (RRH)"](https://www.hudexchange.info/homelessness-assistance/coc-esg-virtual-binders/coc-program-components/permanent-housing/rapid-re-housing/).
- **Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH)**: pairs an **indefinite/long-term rental subsidy** with ongoing supportive services (case management, behavioral health, etc.), and — unlike RRH — generally **requires a qualifying disability** in the household (of an adult or child member) as an eligibility gate, since it's designed for people whose housing stability depends on both a subsidy *and* ongoing services, potentially for life, or until they no longer need the support. [HUD Exchange background, as summarized via search-aggregated sources]; [Jamboree Housing, "What is Permanent Supportive Housing?"](https://www.jamboreehousing.com/blogs/california-affordable-housing/what-is-permanent-supportive-housing).
- In effect: **RRH = short/medium subsidy + search help, no disability requirement, faster exit intended; PSH = indefinite subsidy + ongoing clinical/case-management services, disability required, intended especially for chronically homeless and other high-acuity individuals.** CES/the VI-SPDAT-style acuity score is generally the mechanism used to sort people toward one track or the other (higher acuity → PSH priority; lower acuity → RRH or other lighter-touch resources).
- Agencies/funders: LAHSA (funding/contracting), LA County Housing for Health / DHS (PSH specifically, especially for people with complex medical/behavioral health needs), the Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles (HACLA, vouchers), and a wide range of nonprofit developers/operators (covered in the separate agencies research pass).

### 6. Aftercare / retention

- The clearest, most current, sourced figure found in this research pass: an LA County PSH program has reportedly maintained roughly a **92% 12-month retention rate** for the past decade — meaning the large majority of people placed in PSH remain stably housed at the one-year mark. [ACL/HSRC, "Housing for Health: A Braided Funding Model in LA County, California"](https://acl.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Housing-for-Health-Braided-Funding-Model-California-2025-HSRC.pdf) — a 2025-dated federal case-study document, a solid primary-ish source worth reading in full for a future pass.
- However, that same high-level retention rate coexists with more specific, more troubling findings for a subset of the population: an estimated **5%** of PSH participants are described as needing a higher level of supportive services than the standard PSH model provides, which has led to proposals for an enhanced "PSH+" tier aimed at higher-acuity individuals who are more likely to fall back out of housing under the standard model. [LA2050, "Permanent Supportive Housing Plus (PSH+) pilot program"](https://la2050.org/ideas/2024/permanent-supportive-housing-plus-psh-pilot-program).
- **Racial disparities in PSH retention/exits**: California Policy Lab research specifically documents that Black residents in LA's PSH system experience disproportionate rates of returning to homelessness compared to other groups, and connects this to structural issues in how the CES/PSH matching and support system operates for Black clients specifically — not simply an individual-level outcome. [California Policy Lab, "Inequity in the Permanent Supportive Housing System in Los Angeles: Scale, Scope and Reasons for Black Residents' Returns to Homelessness"](https://capolicylab.org/inequity-in-the-psh-system-in-los-angeles/). A related peer-reviewed piece specifically on premature PSH exits among residents with substance use disorders, with a racial/ethnic disparities lens, is also available: [PMC, "Addressing racial and ethnic disparities in premature exits from permanent supportive housing among residents with substance use disorders"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11773779/).
- A separate, older but directly relevant data point: reporting found that **40% of people helped by a $300 million City of LA homeless initiative had returned to the streets** — this figure's program, exact year, and methodology could not be fully re-confirmed in this pass (it surfaced via a secondary aggregator, not a primary release) and should be checked against the original report before use, but it is consistent with the broader picture that retention/backsliding is uneven across programs and populations, not a uniform 92% success story. [Aggregator citing original reporting — "40 percent of people helped by Los Angeles mayor's $300M homeless project have returned to the streets, report finds"] — flagged as needing primary-source verification.
- A useful predictive/analytical academic source for a deeper future pass: [eScholarship, "Predicting Exits from Permanent Supportive Housing in Los Angeles"](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0p50w569).
- **This research could not find a single authoritative, current, system-wide "percent of all LA homeless-system exits who return to homelessness within N years" figure** that would let the site state one clean headline retention/recidivism statistic — the honest picture from available sources is: retention is genuinely high in the flagship PSH model (~92% at 12 months) for the population that model is designed for, but there is a smaller higher-acuity subgroup and documented racial disparities where returns to homelessness are meaningfully more common, and RRH (as a shorter, lower-subsidy intervention) plausibly has different — likely lower — retention than PSH, though a specific RRH retention rate for LA was not found in this pass.
