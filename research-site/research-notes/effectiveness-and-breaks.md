# Effectiveness and Structural Breakdowns in LA's Homelessness Response

*Research notes compiled 2026-08-01. Every claim below is sourced inline; dates given are publication dates of the source, not necessarily the date of the underlying data. Where evidence is mixed, weak, or actively disputed, that is flagged explicitly rather than presented as settled.*

---

## What Works, What Doesn't

### Housing First as a model — the evidence base

**Strong evidence: Housing First improves housing retention and is cost-competitive.** A systematic review of the U.S. evidence found societal cost savings of about $1.44 for every $1 invested in Housing First programs, with average public-cost savings ranging from $900–$29,400 per person per year after program entry, and consistently higher housing-retention rates than "treatment-first" models — [Housing First: A Review of the Evidence, cited via HUD USER/Journalist's Resource compilation](https://journalistsresource.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Housing-First_-A-Review-of-the-Evidence-_-HUDU-SER.pdf). HUD and the U.S. Interagency Council on Homelessness (USICH) formally designate Housing First a "proven solution" in USICH's 2022 Federal Strategic Plan — [NLIHC evidence summary](https://nlihc.org/sites/default/files/Housing-First-Evidence.pdf).

Locally, RAND's evaluation of the **Just in Reach Pay for Success** program (permanent supportive housing for chronically homeless people cycling through LA County jail) found that, in the year after housing placement, participants had **1.64 fewer emergency room visits** and **more than 4 fewer inpatient hospital days**, and that overall public-service costs (jail, ER, inpatient care, temporary housing) fell by close to 60% relative to a comparison group — offsetting roughly 50–100% of the cost of the housing itself over two years — [RAND, *Just in Reach Pay for Success: Impact Evaluation and Cost Analysis*](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA1758-1.html); [RAND press release, Aug. 2022](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2022/08/16.html). A separate RAND/LA County evaluation found LA County government saved **$1.20 for every $1 spent** on housing and supportive services — [RAND, *A Los Angeles Supportive Housing Program Interrupted the Cycle Between Jail and Homelessness*](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RBA1758-1.html).

**Mixed/disputed: Housing First's record on behavioral health and substance use.** This is the most contested part of the evidence base:
- Proponents (NLIHC, USICH, most peer-reviewed reviews) point to studies showing Housing First participants had equal or better mental-health and substance-use outcomes than "treatment-first" comparison groups, with no evidence Housing First *worsens* those outcomes — [Psychiatric Services, *Housing First Implementation and Key Outcomes Among Homeless Persons With Problematic Substance Use*](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ps.201300195); [At Home/Chez Soi RCT review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679127/).
- Critics — notably the **Cicero Institute** and **Manhattan Institute** — argue Housing First's "no preconditions" design means it does not require or reliably connect people to mental-health and addiction treatment, and that a policy of housing without any behavioral-health mandate is poorly suited to the subset of the unsheltered population with severe untreated mental illness or active addiction — [Cicero Institute, *Housing First Is a Failure*](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/housing-first-is-a-failure/); [Manhattan Institute, *Housing First Is Not the Key to End Homelessness*](https://manhattan.institute/article/housing-first-and-homelessness-the-rhetoric-and-the-reality). Defenders of Housing First respond that Cicero's critique leans on studies with narrow samples applied to populations or claims the original researchers did not make — [Truthout, *A Tech Bro Think Tank Is Trying to Roll Back Evidence-Based Homelessness Policy*](https://truthout.org/articles/a-tech-bro-think-tank-is-trying-to-roll-back-evidence-based-homelessness-policy/).
- **This dispute is not primarily about whether Housing First keeps people housed** (retention rates are well documented and generally strong — see PSH section below) **but about whether housing alone, without mandated treatment, adequately serves people with the most severe behavioral-health needs.** Flag as **disputed**, not settled.

### Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH) — strong evidence for retention and cost offset

- LA County reports that of roughly **25,000 people** who received intensive case management in PSH, **94% retained their housing after one year** — cited via [California Policy Lab / LAHSA KPI reporting](https://capolicylab.org/topics/homelessness/key-performance-indicators-for-homelessness-services-in-los-angeles/).
- A USC Homelessness Policy Research Institute analysis found that of roughly 75,000 Californians placed into PSH, only **8% returned to the streets within 6 months**, versus **23% at 6 months** for people placed into temporary/time-limited subsidized housing — [USC HPRI, *Breaking Cycles of Homelessness: Achieving Stability* (2024)](https://hpri.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Breaking-Cycles-of-Homelessness-Achieving-Stability.pdf).
- The same report documents a significant racial disparity in PSH return-to-homelessness rates: Black single adults returned to homelessness within a year at **14.2%**, nearly double the rate for white (7.3%) and Latino (8%) single adults; the gap for families is even larger (13.5% for Black families vs. 4.2% white / 3.7% Latino) — [USC HPRI (2024)](https://hpri.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Breaking-Cycles-of-Homelessness-Achieving-Stability.pdf).
- Cost: a 2021 LA-based cost study put PSH at roughly **$15,358 per person per year**, against average annual public-service costs of **$40,449** for a comparable unhoused person absent PSH — a reduction of roughly $16,282 in service costs after placement — [cost-effectiveness literature summarized via search of Housing First cost studies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519595/).
- **Rating: strong evidence of effectiveness for retention and cost offset**, specifically for the chronically homeless, high-service-utilization population PSH targets. Evidence is weaker on how well PSH serves people who need more intensive on-site treatment (see Housing First dispute above), and racial disparities in outcomes are a documented, unresolved problem within an otherwise strong-performing model.

### Rapid Re-Housing (RRH) — mixed evidence, with a real mismatch problem

- RRH (called "Time-Limited Subsidy," TLS, in LA County) provides short-term (typically ≤24 months) rental subsidies plus light-touch services, positioned as a cheaper, more scalable alternative to PSH — [LAO/CAO Rapid Re-Housing overview](https://cao.lacity.gov/homeless/hsc20190530f.pdf).
- Where it works: California Policy Lab found RRH "substantially reduced long-term homelessness," with benefits persisting years after the subsidy ended for many participants — [Davis Vanguard summary of CPL findings, 2026](https://davisvanguard.org/2026/07/rapid-rehousing-reduces-homelessness/). The Breaking Barriers RRH program for justice-involved individuals in LA County found **83% of participants remained housed one year after move-in** — [RAND, *Breaking Barriers Rapid Rehousing Program Local Evaluation Report*](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4755-1.html).
- Where it mismatches: RRH assumes participants can absorb full market rent once the subsidy ends. LA County itself now projects **roughly 3,040 households** housed through TLS as of June 2025 will lose that support by June 2026 due to funding reductions — a documented "subsidy cliff" that research links to elevated risk of returning to homelessness, especially for people whose barriers (disability, chronic health conditions, lack of income growth) mean they actually needed PSH-level, permanent support rather than a time-limited bridge — [LA City Homeless Strategy Committee, Time Limited Subsidy report, Sept. 2025](https://cao.lacity.gov/Homeless/HSC/hsc20250911c.pdf); [UC Berkeley Terner Center, *Navigating the Post-Subsidy Cliff*](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/research-and-policy/navigating-the-post-subsidy-cliff-considerations-for-families-approaching-the-end-of-rapid-rehousing/).
- This mismatch is not hypothetical at scale: the **2026 Greater LA Homeless Count increase was directly attributed by LAHSA to a pause in new TLS enrollments and placements**, which caused a system-wide bottleneck (see Part 2 below) — [LA County Homeless Services & Housing, statement on the 2026 count](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/a-statement-on-the-2026-homeless-count/).
- **Rating: mixed evidence.** RRH works well for people whose homelessness is a short-term financial shock; it is documented to fail, or merely delay, homelessness for people with more severe or chronic barriers who need PSH-level support but get routed to RRH instead — often because RRH is cheaper and faster to scale, not because it's the better clinical fit.

### Interim/shelter housing — weak evidence of converting to permanent outcomes

This is one of the most consistently documented weak points in the entire LA system:
- A Los Angeles City Controller audit (released Dec. 2024) found that **fewer than one in five people (under 20%)** in city-funded interim housing over a five-year period moved into permanent housing, and **more than half of people exiting interim housing returned to homelessness or an unknown destination** — [KTLA, summarizing LA City Controller audit, Dec. 2024](https://ktla.com/news/local-news/audit-finds-1-in-4-shelter-beds-in-los-angeles-went-unused-costing-taxpayers-218-million/); [LA City Controller, *Homelessness Audit: Pathways to Permanent Housing*](https://controller.lacity.gov/landings/homeless-pathway-audit).
- The same audit found **roughly 1 in 4 city-funded interim/shelter beds sat unused**, at an estimated cost of **$218 million** over five years — even though occupancy fell well short of LAHSA's 95% target and nearly 1 in 3 people who expressed interest in a shelter bed during FY22–23 could not get one, indicating the vacancy was a matching/operations failure, not a lack of demand — [LA City Controller audit, Dec. 2024](https://controller.lacity.gov/landings/interim-housing-audit).
- A separate, court-ordered independent audit (commissioned under federal Judge David O. Carter's oversight of the *LA Alliance v. City of LA* litigation) found a sampled median permanent-housing exit rate of about **22%** for FY2023-24 interim housing programs, versus **47.8%** of exits that resulted in a return to homelessness — i.e., people were more likely to exit interim housing back onto the street than into permanent housing — [LAist, *Searing audit finds city of LA has failed to properly track billions in homelessness spending*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/audit-homeless-carter-lahsa).
- California Policy Lab's KPI tracking is more optimistic on trend but consistent on scale: interim-to-permanent transitions nearly doubled in FY2021/22 to 4,048 people, and roughly 30% of outreach-engaged individuals exited to interim or permanent housing over a 24-month window — still leaving the majority of people who enter the system without a permanent housing outcome — [California Policy Lab, KPI overview](https://capolicylab.org/topics/homelessness/key-performance-indicators-for-homelessness-services-in-los-angeles/).
- **Rating: weak evidence that interim/shelter housing reliably functions as a bridge to permanent housing** as currently operated in LA. It functions better as crisis stabilization and encampment reduction than as a housing pipeline — the exit data show it is currently more likely to return someone to the street than move them into permanent housing.

### Outreach — moderate reach, low direct conversion to housing

- California Policy Lab analysis of ~37,000 Street Outreach clients found that only **17% were enrolled in interim housing, RRH, or PSH within one year** of first outreach contact, and 17–19% of single adults engaged by outreach moved into interim housing specifically — [California Policy Lab, *Unsheltered in Los Angeles: Insights from Street Outreach Services Data*](https://capolicylab.org/topics/homelessness/unsheltered-in-los-angeles-insights-from-street-outreach-services-data/); [KPI overview](https://capolicylab.org/topics/homelessness/key-performance-indicators-for-homelessness-services-in-los-angeles/).
- A 2019 LA City Controller audit ("Strategy on the Streets") found LAHSA missed most of its own outreach contract goals: it placed only **14%** of assessed individuals in shelter/bridge housing against a 20% goal, and only **4%** in permanent housing against a 10% goal; only 4% of people engaged with a mental-health condition were connected to services, and only 6% of those with substance-use issues were referred to treatment. The audit characterized outreach as reactive — workers spent disproportionate time on encampment-clearing logistics rather than sustained engagement — [ladowntownnews.com summary of controller audit](http://www.ladowntownnews.com/news/audit-slams-lahsa-outreach-strategy/article_69617f6e-c9e7-11e9-9c28-57559b02f5db.html); [LA City Controller, *Strategy on the Streets*](https://controller.lacity.gov/audits/strategy-on-the-streets). *(Note: this audit is from 2019; more recent outreach-specific performance audits were not identified in this research pass — treat pre-pandemic figures as historical baseline, not current performance.)*
- **Rating: weak-to-moderate evidence of direct conversion to housing.** Outreach is a necessary front door and the data-collection backbone of the system (it's where HMIS enrollment typically begins), but on its own it converts a modest share of contacts into housing placements, and historically has underperformed on connecting people to treatment services specifically.

### Prevention and diversion — strong cost-effectiveness evidence, small scale

- LA County's Measure H-funded prevention strategies (evaluated by California Policy Lab) found that only **14.5% of prevention clients returned to homelessness within 12 months** — a strong outcome relative to downstream interventions — [California Policy Lab, *Evaluation of LA County Measure H-Funded Homelessness Prevention Strategies*](https://www.capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Prevention-Evaluation-full-report.pdf).
- Cost comparisons consistently favor prevention: eviction-prevention financial assistance averages roughly **$2,500 per household**, versus up to **$5,000/month** for emergency shelter for the same family, and tens of thousands of dollars per year for PSH once someone has already fallen into chronic homelessness — [cost-of-homelessness fact sheet compilation](https://www.npscoalition.org/post/fact-sheet-cost-of-homelessness).
- The *Just in Reach* jail-diversion program (a form of targeted prevention/diversion) cost roughly **$6,202 per participant per year** in total county service costs and offset 50–100% of its own cost through reduced jail, hospital, and shelter use — [RAND, *Just in Reach Pay for Success*](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA1758-1.html).
- **The catch:** the 2024 California State Auditor found that of five major state homelessness programs it examined (representing $13.7 billion in combined spending), only **two were determined "likely cost-effective"** and **three did not produce enough data to determine effectiveness at all** — meaning even where prevention-style programs are cheap per-unit, the state has not systematically tracked whether spending at scale is working — [CalMatters, *Audit: California fails to track its homelessness spending, outcomes* (April 2024)](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/04/california-homelessness-spending/); California State Auditor, Report 2023-102.2 (referenced via CalMatters and CBS SF coverage, April 2024).
- **Rating: strong evidence of per-unit cost-effectiveness, but scale is small relative to inflow** — LA's prevention system reaches a fraction of the people who become homeless each year, and state-level tracking of prevention-program effectiveness at scale is itself a documented data gap (see Part 2).

## Where & Why It Breaks

### The interim-to-permanent housing bottleneck

This is the central, quantifiable structural failure in the current system, and it has visibly changed the trajectory of homelessness counts in real time:

- In 2025, movement from interim housing to permanent housing fell **36%**, from 12,120 people to 7,770 — a drop of roughly 5,100 fewer people permanently housed that year. Total permanent housing placements county-wide fell from a record **28,625 in 2024 to 23,532 in 2025**.
- The proximate cause: new enrollments and permanent placements through the **Time-Limited Subsidy (RRH) program** were paused, and the number of people newly housed via TLS dropped **36%**, from 10,308 (2024) to 6,590 (2025).
- The downstream effect: movement from interim housing *back to the street* rose **24%** (4,708 → 5,842 people), while inflow from the street into interim housing kept coming (down only 8%, from 16,724 to 15,380) — meaning interim housing kept filling from the front while its back door to permanent housing narrowed.
- Consequence: after two consecutive years of decline, the **2026 Greater LA Homeless Count showed an increase** — LA County homelessness up 1.2% to ~73,040, and City of LA homelessness up 3.4% to ~45,194; unsheltered homelessness specifically rose 3.3% county-wide and 7.9% in the city. LAHSA's own public statement attributed the reversal to the TLS enrollment pause — [LA County Homeless Services & Housing, statement on the 2026 count](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/a-statement-on-the-2026-homeless-count/); [LAist, *LA homelessness rose in 2026, official count shows*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homelessness-rose-in-2026-official-count-shows/).
- This is a textbook case of the "bottleneck" the research questions asked about: the system had capacity to move people *into* interim housing (via Inside Safe encampment resolution and shelter expansion) faster than it had funded capacity to move them *out* into permanent housing — and when the permanent-housing funding pipeline (TLS) was interrupted even temporarily, the backlog immediately reversed hard-won gains.

### Exits back into homelessness — the "funding cliff" and inadequate step-down support

- The RRH/TLS "subsidy cliff" is well documented: roughly 3,040 LA County households housed via TLS as of mid-2025 were projected to lose that support by mid-2026 due to federal, state, and county funding reductions, well before most participants' incomes could realistically absorb full market rent — [LA City Homeless Strategy Committee, Time-Limited Subsidy report, Sept. 2025](https://cao.lacity.gov/Homeless/HSC/hsc20250911c.pdf).
- Independent audit data corroborate that exits-to-homelessness now outpace exits-to-permanent-housing in a sampled set of interim housing programs (47.8% vs. 22.0%, FY2023-24) — [LAist coverage of the Carter-ordered independent audit](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/audit-homeless-carter-lahsa).
- Racial disparity compounds the funding-cliff problem: Black clients return to homelessness after PSH exit at roughly double the rate of white or Latino clients, suggesting the "step-down" support structure (or the housing/services match itself) is not equally effective across populations — [USC HPRI (2024)](https://hpri.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Breaking-Cycles-of-Homelessness-Achieving-Stability.pdf).
- Mayor Bass's **Inside Safe** program — the city's flagship encampment-to-interim-housing initiative — illustrates the exit problem directly: as of a March 2025 LAHSA-cited report, the program had moved roughly 2,571 people into interim housing across 43 encampment operations, but of the 609 people who had since exited, about 366 (roughly 60%) returned to homelessness; only about 500 people total had been permanently housed via Inside Safe by that point, at a cost that had by then reportedly topped **$322 million** — [Knock LA, *Inside Safe: Limited Success and Ongoing Controversy*](https://knock-la.com/inside-safe-controversy-limited-success-reducing-homelessness/); [Westside Current, *Bass to End Homeless Emergency as Inside Safe Costs Top $322 Million With Meager Results*](https://www.westsidecurrent.com/news/bass-to-end-homeless-emergency-as-inside-safe-costs-top-322-million-with-meager-results/article_33f507f8-399f-451e-867b-8ca003852939.html).

### Interagency handoff failures and institutional collapse at LAHSA

The clearest, most consequential "falls between agencies" story in this research pass is not a single client case but the collapse of the coordinating agency itself:

- A federal judge, David O. Carter, overseeing the *LA Alliance v. City of Los Angeles* homelessness litigation, ordered an independent audit of city and county homelessness spending. Released audits described the tracking of roughly **$2.4 billion** in city homelessness spending over four years as effectively impossible to verify, called the situation a "slow train wreck," and found more than **$50 million** in cash advances to service providers that the county had not recouped, plus a pattern of late payments and poorly monitored contracts — [LAist, *Judge blasts LA homeless spending as a 'train wreck' and threatens to seize control*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/judge-blasts-la-homeless-spending-as-a-train-wreck-and-threatens-to-seize-control); [LAist, *Searing audit finds city of LA has failed to properly track billions in homelessness spending*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/audit-homeless-carter-lahsa).
- Separately, a 2025 county-commissioned financial review found LAHSA could not produce documentation verifying the existence of nearly **2,300 housing sites** it was nominally responsible for — [CalMatters, *What the implosion of LA homeless agency reveals about CA crisis* (May 2025)](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2025/05/la-homeless-agency/).
- In April 2025, the LA County Board of Supervisors voted to terminate the county's contract with LAHSA and redirect roughly **$300 million/year** in funding to a new county homelessness department, following the audits above. LAHSA subsequently laid off 284 employees — [CalMatters (May 2025)](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2025/05/la-homeless-agency/).
- In June 2026, HUD went further and **suspended federal homelessness funding to LAHSA**, citing what HUD's Office of Inspector General characterized as repeated false statements and a failure to maintain financial controls and conflict-of-interest safeguards — [HUD.gov, *HUD Cuts Off Fraud-Filled Los Angeles Homelessness Funding*](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-048); [CalMatters, *Trump administration blocks federal homelessness funds in LA* (June 2026)](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2026/06/lahsa-hud-funding/).
- A separate, longer-running example: between 2015–2020, three LA-area agencies — LAHSA (~$29M), the Housing Authority of the City of LA (~$82M), and the LA County Development Authority (~$38M) — collectively **returned nearly $150 million in unspent federal homeless grants to HUD**, citing HUD's "rigid" funding rules, a shortage of workable housing units and willing landlords, and high program attrition among clients — a case of money sitting stuck at agency boundaries rather than reaching people — [PBS SoCal / ABC7 / CBS LA coverage of the report](https://www.pbssocal.org/news-community/l-a-agencies-returned-nearly-150-million-in-unspent-federal-homeless-grants/).
- The July 2025 Homeless Count itself became a case study in institutional distrust: LAist reported that in the days before releasing the 2025 count, LAHSA revised its data to exclude more than 2,300 observations and remove 437 sheltered individuals from the tally without clear public reconciliation — changes that increased the reported year-over-year decline in the city's homeless count from 2.5% to 3.4%. **This is a disputed/contested claim about data integrity, not a confirmed finding of intentional manipulation** — flagged here because it bears directly on how much confidence to place in LAHSA's self-reported outcome trends generally — [World Socialist Web Site summary of LAist reporting, July 2025](https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/2025/07/20/xpmk-j20.html) *(secondary source citing LAist's original reporting; treat the underlying LAist investigation as the primary source of record)*.

### Data siloing between HMIS and provider systems

- LA/OC HMIS is the county's shared client database, legally required for most federally/state-funded providers, but it is **not universal**: providers who don't receive HMIS-covered funding aren't required to participate, and Victim Service Providers (domestic-violence shelters, etc.) are legally barred from entering client data into HMIS at all, meaning a meaningful share of service contacts are invisible to system-wide tracking — [LAHSA, *HMIS* overview](https://www.lahsa.org/hmis/about).
- Client intake in the City of LA historically occurred at three separate points of entry (central call center, street outreach teams, and shelters), with intake data "continuously updated" as clients move between providers rather than unified at first contact — a structural source of duplicate intakes and inconsistent case histories — [Hilton Foundation, *Los Angeles Homeless Data Assessment Report: Issues and Recommendations*](https://www.hiltonfoundation.org/learning/los-angeles-homeless-data-assessment-report-issues-and-recommendations/).
- The county's own newer Measure A performance-metrics effort was explicitly designed to solve this: its "linked database" approach was framed by the county as integrating data "previously siloed between different County agencies" — an implicit admission that, prior to Measure A (passed Nov. 2024), county agencies could not reliably see a shared, cross-agency picture of an individual's status in the system — [California Policy Lab, *Measure A Baselines Report* (Jan. 2025)](https://capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Data-Subcommittee-Baselines-report.pdf); [LA County Homeless Services & Housing, Measure A Resource Hub](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/measure-a-hub/).
- The coordinated entry system (CES) — meant to be the unified "front door" matching people to housing resources — has been reported to function less as a single queue than as a series of sequential gateways ("a second and third line stacked behind the first"), with no guaranteed match and PSH wait times ranging from weeks to years; reporting has also found CES prioritization under-serves Black unhoused residents relative to need — [Knock LA, *Inside LA's Endless Queues for Housing, Care, and Survival*](https://knock-la.com/inside-la-endless-queues-for-housing/).

### Workforce: turnover, burnout, and continuity of care

- Most frontline homeless-response workers in LA earn **$40,000–$60,000/year**, while it takes roughly $64,000/year to afford a one-bedroom apartment in LA County without exceeding 30% of income on housing — meaning a meaningful share of the workforce housing other people is itself rent-burdened or housing-insecure — [LAist, *Homelessness Outreach Workers In LA Are Exhausted And Stressed Out*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/homelessness-outreach-workers-in-la-are-exhausted-and-stressed-out); [KQED, *Underpaid and Burned Out*](https://www.kqed.org/news/11902904/underpaid-and-burned-out-many-outreach-workers-for-unhoused-californians-are-leaving-their-jobs).
- At Chrysalis, a major LA homeless-services nonprofit, case-worker turnover runs around **30%**, with 42% of departing staff citing insufficient pay as their reason for leaving. At the People Concern, newer staff tend to stay only 12–18 months.
- Caseloads reported in the reporting reviewed ranged from roughly **26 to 30 clients per case manager**.
- Direct consequence for clients: reporting found some homeless clients in LA County have cycled through **six or seven different case managers**, undermining the relationship-building that outreach and case management depend on — [LAist / KQED reporting, as above].
- **Rating: this is one of the more clearly evidenced, least-disputed structural breaks** — pay, turnover, and caseload data point consistently in the same direction, and the mechanism (new case manager every few months → no continuity of trust) is straightforward and well documented, even though rigorous causal studies isolating turnover's effect on housing outcomes specifically were not identified in this pass.

### Funding-cycle mismatches and construction timelines for PSH

- Proposition HHH (the 2016 city bond measure) illustrates the mismatch between how homelessness capital is funded and how long supportive housing actually takes to deliver: individual projects have taken **three to six years** from funding to opening, with most of the original cohort not opening until 2023–2026.
- Per-unit costs rose sharply over that build-out: the original 2016 estimate was **$350,000** (studio/1BR) to **$414,000** (2BR+); by 2019 the average had exceeded **$502,000**; for projects still in construction, average per-unit cost rose from **$531,000 (2020) to $596,846 (2021)**; some individual units were audited at up to **$837,000** — [LAmag, *An Audit Lays Out What's Gone Wrong with L.A.'s Homeless Housing Measure*](https://lamag.com/urbandevelopment/proposition-hhh-audit/); [ABC7, *Audit of LA's homelessness measure shows cost per housing unit as much as $837,000*](https://abc7.com/post/prop-hhh-housing-los-angeles-audit/11600642/); [LA City Controller, *The High Cost of Homeless Housing: Review of Proposition HHH*](https://controller.lacity.gov/audits/high-cost-of-homeless-housing-hhh).
- Consequence: the original target of **10,000 supportive housing units** was cut to **5,873** — a 41% reduction — without HHH's total bond authorization changing, because rising per-unit cost consumed the same pool of money faster than expected — [LA City Controller audit, as above].
- This is the structural core of the "funding cycle mismatch" the research asked about: HHH was a one-time bond passed in 2016 against a fixed dollar ceiling, while construction costs, permitting timelines, and land costs kept rising over the 3–6 year build cycle — meaning the funding instrument's fixed size was already mismatched to real delivery costs and timelines before ground was even broken on many projects.

### NIMBY and political resistance as a measurable friction point

- Even though voters approved Prop HHH and Measure H by roughly 77% margins county/city-wide, individual proposed shelter and PSH sites have repeatedly drawn organized local opposition — e.g., a 154-bed shelter in Venice was the subject of a 2019 lawsuit by the Venice Stakeholders Association, and a proposed Koreatown shelter drew large community rallies after residents said the site was chosen without their input — [NPR, *LA Homeless Shelters Face Opposition* (2018)](https://www.npr.org/2018/06/30/624911798/la-homeless-shelters-face-opposition).
- Structural enabler for the city: shelters sited on city-owned land can bypass standard zoning review, which is part of how the city has been able to build interim housing at all despite local opposition — but this workaround does not extend to most privately developed PSH, and roughly 80% of the city's residential land remains zoned single-family, sharply limiting where multi-family PSH can be built as-of-right — [NPR (2018), as above].
- Reporting also documents opposition specifically to *permanent* supportive housing (as distinct from temporary shelters), sometimes grounded in resident concerns about crime, which developers and researchers dispute is a consistent effect of PSH siting — this causal claim (that PSH increases neighborhood crime) is contested in the literature and should be treated as a disputed community perception rather than a documented outcome in this research pass.
- **Net effect:** NIMBY resistance functions less as a single quantifiable delay-in-days and more as a recurring tax on siting — driving up legal costs, forcing site changes, and (per the HHH cost data above) contributing to the broader pattern of rising per-unit costs and shrinking total unit counts over time, though isolating NIMBY's specific dollar or time contribution separate from permitting/construction-cost inflation generally was not possible from the sources reviewed here.

## Additional cross-cutting findings

- **State-level accountability gap:** The 2024 California State Auditor report found California could not account for how **$24 billion** spent on homelessness from FY2018–19 through FY2022–23 improved outcomes, in part because the state's own coordinating body (Cal ICH) had not analyzed spending data past 2021. Only 2 of 5 major programs examined ($13.7B combined) were found "likely cost-effective"; the other 3 lacked sufficient data to judge — [CalMatters, *Audit: California fails to track its homelessness spending, outcomes* (April 12, 2024)](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/04/california-homelessness-spending/); California State Auditor, Report 2023-102.2 (April 2024).
- **Homeless mortality is falling, but from a very high base:** LA County recorded 2,208 deaths of unhoused people in 2024, the first year-over-year decline since county tracking began in 2014 (down ~300 from 2023; mortality rate fell 10%, from 3,326 to 2,994 per 100,000). Overdose remains the leading cause of death (40% of deaths in 2024, down from 45% in 2023), and county officials attributed the improvement partly to expanded overdose-prevention and harm-reduction services — though the county still averages roughly six homeless deaths per day — [LA County Dept. of Public Health, March 2026 release](https://lacounty.gov/2026/03/10/los-angeles-county-homeless-mortality-rate-decreases-for-the-first-time/).
- **The 2025 "decline" and 2026 "increase" in the homeless count are both contested to some degree** in how they were measured, not just what they show: independent RAND field research found LAHSA's official count undercounts unsheltered people in surveyed "hot spot" neighborhoods (Skid Row, Hollywood, Venice) by a widening margin — 26% in 2024, 32% in 2025 — and found rough sleeping specifically rose sharply (up 20% year over year, reaching 44% of the unsheltered population in the study area by the 2026 count window) even during the two years the official count showed an overall decline — [LAist, *LA's official homeless tally increasingly undercounts people on the streets, RAND study finds*](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/lahsa-homeless-count-rand-accuracy-2025-volunteers-report-study). This is flagged as a **methodological dispute**, not a resolved fact: it means the official annual count and independent field research have diverged, and both cannot be fully correct simultaneously.
