# LA Homelessness Interventions: Case Studies of What Was Tried

Research notes compiled 2026-08-01. Each program below is documented with scale, cost, and outcomes as reported in primary sources (city/county/state documents, court filings) and rigorous journalism/research (RAND, UCLA, CalMatters, LAist, state and city audits). Figures often come from different points in time as programs evolved — dates are noted next to each claim. Where sources conflict or data is incomplete, that is noted explicitly rather than resolved by guesswork.

---

## Project Roomkey (2020 COVID-era hotel/motel shelter leasing)

**What it was:** A California program launched in April 2020 using FEMA/CARES Act funding to lease hotel and motel rooms statewide so people experiencing homelessness — especially those medically vulnerable to COVID-19 — could isolate individually instead of in congregate shelters. Los Angeles City and County were among the largest participants.

**Scale:** Statewide, roughly 62,000 people were served through more than 300 hotels, about 16,000 rooms, and 1,300 trailers across 55 counties and three tribes ([Wikipedia, Project Roomkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Roomkey); [CA Health Care Foundation, 2020](https://www.chcf.org/resource/2020/07/24/californias-project-roomkey/)). LAHSA reported Project Roomkey had ended homelessness for 4,824 people in LA specifically by the time it wound down ([LAHSA news release](https://www.lahsa.org/news?article=901-project-roomkey-ends-homelessness-for-4-824-people)).

**Cost:** As of March 2021, statewide spending was about $59 million, averaging $35,308 per room over roughly 10 months, or about $117 per room per day ([search-aggregated reporting citing state DOF/CalOES data](https://dof.ca.gov/media/docs/budget/covid-19-information/covid-19-direct-disaster-response-allocations-and-notifications/prk_rehousing_strategy-final.pdf)). FEMA agreed in January 2021 to reimburse 100% of eligible costs, but later imposed a 20-day cap on reimbursable stays (effective June 11, 2021–May 11, 2023), leaving state and local governments exposed to more than $300 million in costs for people who stayed longer ([CalMatters, Feb. 2024](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/02/fema-roomkey-october-letter/)).

**Outcomes:** An outcomes evaluation summarized by CalMatters (May 2024) found that after leaving Roomkey, roughly one-third of participants moved to permanent or other temporary housing, about a quarter entered other emergency shelter, 15% returned to unsheltered homelessness, and 18% had unknown destinations ([CalMatters, May 2024](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/05/california-homeless-project-roomkey/); [LAist brief on the same evaluation](https://laist.com/brief/news/housing-homelessness/californias-covid-homeless-shelter-evaluation-results)).

**Wind-down:** Permanent or interim housing was offered to all remaining participants as the last sites ramped down by February 2023; the program's population and functions were largely absorbed into Project Homekey and, later, Inside Safe ([Governing, "Project Roomkey... too expensive to last"](https://www.governing.com/urban/californias-project-roomkey-for-the-unhoused-was-a-success-but-too-expensive-to-last); [PPIC blog, lessons from Roomkey](https://www.ppic.org/blog/what-lessons-can-be-learned-from-project-roomkey/)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** It worked as an emergency public-health measure — getting large numbers of people off the street fast during a pandemic — but was never designed as a housing-exit pathway, so only about a third of participants moved to any form of housing afterward, and the FEMA reimbursement cliff made it fiscally unsustainable as an ongoing shelter model.

**Verdict:** Mixed — effective, low-friction emergency sheltering at scale during COVID, but a poor permanent-housing pipeline on its own (roughly two-thirds of exits were not to permanent housing) and financially unsustainable once federal reimbursement was capped.

## Project Homekey (state-funded hotel/motel-to-permanent-housing conversion)

**What it was:** California's follow-on to Roomkey, launched by Governor Newsom in summer 2020, providing grants for local governments and developers to acquire and convert hotels, motels, and other buildings into interim or permanent housing, rather than just leasing rooms.

**Scale:** By early 2024 the state had funded more than 15,000 units statewide, fully committing the initial $3.5 billion, and total Homekey funding has grown to over $3.8 billion, later supplemented by "Homekey+" ($2.2 billion, launched September 2024, targeting veterans and people with mental health/substance use needs) ([Planetizen, May 2026, citing state data](https://www.planetizen.com/news/2026/05/137536-californias-hotel-housing-program-put-13500-people-homes); [Housing Matters SD summary](https://www.housingmatterssd.org/from-hotels-to-homes-inside-californias-3-5b-homekey-initiative/)). Planetizen (May 2026) reports the program has put roughly 13,500 people into homes to date.

**LA-specific numbers:** Within the City of Los Angeles, Homekey projects total roughly 176 interim and permanent units ([Mayor's office announcements aggregated](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/city-los-angeles-secures-state-funding-four-housing-sites-nearly-200-units-permanent-and)). LA County has used Homekey to acquire 32 properties (2,157 units) — mostly hotels/motels — for conversion to interim and permanent housing ([homeless.lacounty.gov, Homekey program page](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/homekey/); [LA County, "14 Hotels to Become Housing with Homekey 2.0"](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/14-hotels-to-become-housing-with-homekey-2-0/)).

**Cost per unit:** The California State Auditor's April 2024 report (2023-102.1) reviewed a sample of 8 Homekey projects and found an average cost of about $144,000 per unit for Round 1 projects (2020-2021) — ranging from about $90,000/unit in Fresno to about $200,000/unit in San Francisco — versus $380,000-$570,000 for newly built affordable housing in California in 2019, making Homekey "likely" cost-effective by comparison ([California State Auditor Report 2023-102.1, April 2024](https://www.auditor.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/2023-102.1-Report.pdf); [CalMatters coverage](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/04/california-homelessness-spending/)).

**Oversight gap:** CalMatters' investigative piece (May 2026), built on more than 100 public-records requests, found that the state never systematically tracked which Homekey projects succeeded, stalled, or failed outright; a 2026 state bill to mandate a comprehensive Homekey audit died in the legislature. CalMatters describes LA-area results as "boom-or-bust" — some conversions succeeded quickly while others remain stuck in limbo years later, with no public accounting of the failure rate ([CalMatters, "Did Newsom's $3.8 billion hotels-to-housing program pay off?", May 7, 2026](https://calmatters.org/housing/2026/05/newsom-homekey-records/)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** Acquisition-and-conversion is genuinely cheaper and faster than ground-up construction, which is Homekey's core structural advantage over Measure HHH. But because the state distributed money to local grantees with minimal standardized reporting requirements and no statewide audit mandate, nobody — including CalMatters using public records law — can currently produce a reliable statewide or LA-specific success/failure rate for conversions.

**Verdict:** Mixed — the cost-per-unit math is a real success relative to new construction, but the program's near-total lack of outcome tracking and accountability makes claims of overall program success (or failure) impossible to verify independently as of mid-2026.

## Measure HHH-funded permanent supportive housing construction

**What it was:** A $1.2 billion general obligation bond approved by LA City voters in November 2016, promising roughly 10,000 units of permanent supportive housing (with construction financing layered with other funding sources) to address chronic and mental-illness/substance-use-linked homelessness.

**Cost per unit (the national talking point):** LA City Controller audits tracked steadily rising costs. The Controller's earlier reviews found average per-unit development costs rising from about $507,000 (2019 projects) to about $531,000-$559,000 (2020), with some individual projects reaching as high as $746,000-$837,000 per unit ([ABC7, "Audit of LA's homelessness measure shows cost per housing unit as much as $837,000"](https://abc7.com/post/prop-hhh-housing-los-angeles-audit/11600642/); [Courthouse News, audit coverage](https://www.courthousenews.com/audit-la-homelessness-support-efforts-plagued-by-delays-high-costs/); [Urbanize LA, "Costs Rise for Measure HHH Supportive Housing Developments"](https://la.urbanize.city/post/costs-rise-measure-hhh-supportive-housing-developments)). More recent LA Housing Department figures cited in 2025 point to a lower blended average of roughly $135,000 in HHH-specific investment per unit for units still in the pipeline (HHH funds are typically one funding layer among several, so this figure is not the same as total development cost per unit) ([SCANPH response to the Controller's 2025 audit](http://www.scanph.org/policy-roundup-blog/scanph-response-to-latest-hhh-audit-from-city-controller)).

**Units delivered vs. the 10,000-unit promise:** As of the most recent tally located, roughly 8,091 total units (about 6,578 of them permanent supportive housing) are in the HHH pipeline across 125 projects; only about 14% (roughly 1,100-1,300 units) were reported ready for occupancy, with 54% still in construction and 32% in pre-development — nearly a decade after voters approved the bond ([search-aggregated data citing LAHD's HHH Progress Dashboard](https://housing.lacity.gov/housing/hhh-progress-dashboard)). This falls well short of both the original 10,000-unit promise and the original timeline, which projected most units would open years earlier.

**Timeline delays:** City Controller audits (the office has issued at least three annual HHH audits, plus a 2025 review by Controller Kenneth Mejia, "The Problems and Progress of Prop. HHH") repeatedly cite permitting delays, financing "stacking" complexity (HHH funds are typically only ~20-40% of a project's capital stack, requiring developers to separately assemble tax credits, state grants, and other financing before breaking ground), and construction-cost inflation as the primary drivers of both cost overruns and multi-year delays ([LA Controller, "The High Cost of Homeless Housing: Review of Proposition HHH"](https://controller.lacity.gov/audits/high-cost-of-homeless-housing-hhh); [LA Controller, "The Problems and Progress of Prop. HHH"](https://controller.lacity.gov/audits/problems-and-progress-of-prop-hhh); [Urbanize LA, audit summary](https://la.urbanize.city/post/audit-recommends-reallocation-measure-hhh-supportive-housing-funds)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** HHH's core design flaw, per the Controller's audits, is that a housing bond alone doesn't fund a project — it has to wait in line for other financing (tax credits, cap-and-trade dollars, etc.), which is what stretched timelines to 3-6 years or more per project and let per-unit costs balloon with LA's construction-cost inflation. It also funded 100% permanent, ground-up construction — the most expensive and slowest form of housing delivery — rather than the acquisition/conversion approach Homekey used.

**Verdict:** Mostly didn't work as promised — roughly a decade after passage, the program has produced a small fraction of the promised 10,000 units on the original timeline, at per-unit costs that became a national symbol of homeless-housing cost overruns, though a growing share of the pipeline is now finally reaching completion.

## Inside Safe (Mayor Karen Bass, launched December 2022)

**What it is:** Mayor Bass's signature encampment-resolution strategy, launched on her first day tackling a homelessness state of emergency she declared upon taking office. The model: city outreach teams and LAHSA negotiate with an entire encampment at once, offering everyone motel/hotel rooms (interim housing) on the spot, with a goal of eventually moving people into permanent housing while preventing the site from being reoccupied.

**Scale over time (figures reported at different dates, cumulative):**
- By October 2024 (CalMatters/LAist): 67 encampments cleared, 3,254 people moved into hotels, with about 23% having moved on to permanent housing by that point — an improvement from just 7 months after launch, when fewer than 6% of people in Inside Safe hotels had reached permanent housing ([CalMatters, "LA has a different solution to homeless camps. But it's not working for everyone," Oct. 28, 2024](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/10/inside-safe/); [LAist republication](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-has-a-different-solution-to-homeless-camps-but-its-not-working-for-everyone)).
- As of October 2025: Inside Safe had served 5,179 people across 114 encampment operations; 1,243 were in permanent housing, 1,636 remained in interim placements, and 2,300 had exited the program with the city providing no public breakdown of what happened to them. In the four months prior, 249 participants had returned to homelessness and 18 had died ([Westside Current, "Bass to End Homeless Emergency as Inside Safe Costs Top $322 Million With Meager Results"](https://www.westsidecurrent.com/news/bass-to-end-homeless-emergency-as-inside-safe-costs-top-322-million-with-meager-results/article_33f507f8-399f-451e-867b-8ca003852939.html)).
- By mid-2026 reporting: of roughly 6,000 people served cumulatively since 2023, about 40% had exited the program and returned to unsheltered homelessness ([LA Public Press, July 2026](https://lapublicpress.org/2026/07/la-homeless-count-2026-results-bass-unhoused/)).

**Cost:** The city has spent or committed more than $322 million on Inside Safe as of late 2025/2026 reporting. Divided across the 1,243 people reported in permanent housing at that point, that is roughly $259,000 per person actually permanently housed; averaged across all 5,179 people served regardless of outcome, it is roughly $62,000 per person ([Westside Current, cited above](https://www.westsidecurrent.com/news/bass-to-end-homeless-emergency-as-inside-safe-costs-top-322-million-with-meager-results/article_33f507f8-399f-451e-867b-8ca003852939.html)). An earlier NBC LA investigation (examining a narrower early period) found $67 million spent for only 255 people permanently housed at that point ([NBC LA investigation](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/investigations/la-mayor-inside-safe-homeless-housing-program/3281201/)).

**"Whack-a-mole" criticism:** Multiple outlets and researchers have documented that clearing one encampment often leads to displacement to nearby blocks rather than disappearance of unsheltered homelessness, requiring repeat operations in the same corridors (e.g., city crews returning to Westlake after new encampments re-formed) ([NBC LA, "The Big Shuffle"](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/the-big-shuffle-some-homeless-people-in-la-being-shuffled-from-block-to-block/3397783/)). A RAND longitudinal study (LA LEADS, tracking Hollywood, Venice, and Skid Row from 2021 onward) found that while tent dwelling fell by roughly half since 2021 (a 23% single-year drop in 2025), rough sleeping and vehicle dwelling rose to offset it — rough sleeping specifically hit a four-year high — and nearly half of surveyed rough sleepers reported losing a dwelling in the past year, with 46% of those saying it was confiscated or towed by government officials or service providers ([RAND, "Homelessness Holds Steady Across Three L.A. Neighborhoods as Rough Sleeping Reaches a Four-Year High," May 2026](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2026/05/homelessness-holds-steady-across-three-la-neighborhoods.html); [RAND LA LEADS 2024 Annual Report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA1890-6.html)). RAND's own commentary summarized the finding as: encampment sweeps and camping-ban-style enforcement reduce visible tent homelessness in the short term, but push people into harder-to-track and often more dangerous forms of unsheltered homelessness rather than into housing ([RAND, "Los Angeles' Homelessness Strategy Needs a Next Act," June 2026](https://www.rand.org/pubs/commentary/2026/06/los-angeles-homelessness-strategy-needs-a-next-act.html)).

**Emergency declaration lifted:** In November 2025, Bass lifted the local homelessness state of emergency she had declared on her first day in office, citing a second straight year of LAHSA-reported declines and a 17% drop in street homelessness, while acknowledging "the crisis remains" ([reporting on the lifting of the emergency, cited in Westside Current](https://www.westsidecurrent.com/news/bass-to-end-homeless-emergency-as-inside-safe-costs-top-322-million-with-meager-results/article_33f507f8-399f-451e-867b-8ca003852939.html)). That decline reversed in the 2026 Point-in-Time count (released July 24, 2026), which found a 3.4% increase in city homelessness (to 45,194) and a 7.9% jump in unsheltered homelessness in the city, erasing two years of reported progress — a change LAHSA attributed largely to a pause in new enrollments and permanent placements tied to Time-Limited Subsidies ([LAist, "LA homelessness rose in 2026, official count shows," July 2026](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/la-homelessness-rose-in-2026-official-count-shows); [HeySoCal, July 2026](https://heysocal.com/2026/07/25/after-2-years-of-reductions-2026-count-reveals-increase-in-la-homelessness/)).

**Transparency criticism:** Reporting repeatedly flags that the city does not publicly disaggregate what happens to the roughly 40-45% of participants who "exit" the program — whether to housing elsewhere, other cities, jail, or death — making independent verification of the program's real success rate difficult.

**Why it worked/didn't:** Inside Safe's motel-voucher model removes the friction of shelter-bed matching (an entire encampment moves at once), which is its real innovation over A Bridge Home. But because interim placements aren't paired reliably with a fast permanent-housing pipeline (the same bottleneck that has plagued HHH and Homekey), a large share of participants stall in interim housing or cycle back to the street, and the program has not stopped encampments from re-forming in cleared areas.

**Verdict:** Mixed — real, verifiable reductions in visible tent encampments and several thousand people moved indoors at least temporarily, but a high and rising return-to-homelessness rate (around 40%), incomplete outcome transparency, and independent RAND research showing displacement into harder-to-see forms of homelessness rather than net reduction.

## Tiny home villages (Hope of the Valley Rescue Mission and others)

**What it is:** Prefabricated single-occupancy or double-occupancy shed-style units (roughly 8x8 to 8x16 feet) clustered into managed "villages" with shared bathrooms, meals, and case management — pitched as faster and more dignified than congregate shelter, and cheaper than permanent construction. Hope of the Valley Rescue Mission is the primary operator of LA's tiny home sites.

**Scale:** LA's first site (Chandler Street, North Hollywood) opened with 40 homes/75 beds; a later, much larger North Hollywood-area complex reached 103 units/200 beds, and by some counts the North Hollywood-area sites collectively total 326 beds ([LAist, "First Tiny Home Community Opens In LA"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/first-tiny-home-community-opens-in-la-with-75-beds-homeless); [NBC LA, "Highland Park Tiny Home Village...Largest in US"](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/highland-park-tiny-home-village-homeless/2726687/)).

**Cost:** A 39-home North Hollywood village cost $5.2 million to set up — individual units cost about $7,500 each including labor/materials, but the bulk of the cost went to re-routing water, power, and sewer infrastructure to the site. By comparison, the city of Riverside built a 30-unit tiny home village for about $514,000 total, illustrating how much LA-specific site/infrastructure costs (not the units themselves) drive up costs ([A-Mark Foundation / Pepperdine analysis, "What Are the Costs of Building Tiny Home Villages"](https://amarkfoundation.org/reports/tiny-home-villages/)).

**Move-on rate:** A-Mark Foundation/Pepperdine analysis of North Hollywood-area villages found an average of 11-14 people per month moving from those communities (326 total beds) into permanent housing ([A-Mark Foundation, "How Much Does It Cost to Operate Tiny Home Villages"](https://amarkfoundation.org/reports/how-much-does-it-cost-to-operate-tiny-home-villages-for-people-experiencing-homelessness/)). Citywide, the LA Controller's "Pathways to Permanent Housing" audit (covering all interim housing types, including tiny homes, 2019-2023) found that fewer than one in five people in any interim housing type — including tiny homes — secured permanent housing, and more than half of people leaving interim housing returned to homelessness or an unknown destination ([LA Controller interim housing audit landing page](https://controller.lacity.gov/landings/interim-housing-audit); [KTLA coverage](https://ktla.com/news/local-news/audit-finds-1-in-4-shelter-beds-in-los-angeles-went-unused-costing-taxpayers-218-million/)).

**Criticism:** Sharp criticism has focused on overall construction cost relative to unit size, complaints that units are too small, and resident complaints about lack of privacy, limited access to services and employment support, and restrictive rules — one resident compared the experience to "living in a dog pound" ([search-aggregated resident reporting](https://medium.com/@kirsten.cintigo/tiny-homes-in-the-valley-understanding-resedas-tiny-home-village-36e3ae3d50e2)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** The unit cost itself is genuinely low, but LA's site-prep and utility infrastructure costs erase most of that advantage versus other cities. As with other interim-housing types, tiny homes function as a holding pattern rather than a housing solution unless paired with a working permanent-housing pipeline — which the city-wide audit shows is not reliably happening.

**Verdict:** Mixed — cheap and fast to deploy relative to construction, and does get people off the street into a supervised setting, but move-on-to-permanent-housing rates are low and city infrastructure costs undercut the model's cost advantage.

## Safe parking programs

**What it is:** Supervised overnight parking lots (often at churches, government lots, or business parking) for people sheltering in their vehicles, paired with restroom access, security, and case management. Safe Parking LA is the largest operator.

**Scale:** Safe Parking LA operates roughly six lots and describes itself as the largest safe-parking network in the region and the only organization in LA fully dedicated to vehicular homelessness ([CHA/Catholic Health World profile, Nov. 2025](https://www.chausa.org/news-and-publications/publications/catholic-health-world/archives/november-2025/safe-parking-la-gives-people-who-are-unhoused-a-secure-place-to-sleep-in-their-vehicles-overnight)). This is a small fraction of need: people living in vehicles make up more than 44% of LA County's unsheltered homeless population ([Smart Cities Dive, safe parking coverage](https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/safe-parking-programs-offer-support-to-people-living-in-their-cars/735720/)).

**Effectiveness:** Safe Parking LA reports having stabilized more than 2,500 vehicle-dwellers since its founding, with 45% of participants self-resolving their homelessness or moving into interim/permanent housing ([Lido Consulting summary of program data](https://www.lidoconsulting.com/post/safe-parking-programs-an-emerging-philanthropic-investment-to-tackle-homelessness); peer-reviewed analysis: [Housing Policy Debate, "An Analysis of Safe Parking Programs," 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10511482.2024.2313511)).

**Budget threat:** In the FY2026-27 city budget process, LA City Council initially proposed defunding roughly 11 safe parking sites; Councilmember Szabo identified $1.29 million to restore funding for 5 sites and an additional $1.7 million to save 6 more, preserving the program for one more year after public pressure ([LA Public Press, May 2026](https://lapublicpress.org/2026/05/la-homelessness-safe-parking-city-council/); [Invisible People, budget-cut coverage](https://invisiblepeople.tv/safe-parking-lots-offer-stability-to-working-angelenos-living-in-cars-budget-cuts-could-end-them/)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** Because it targets a distinct sub-population (people who already have a vehicle and often some income/employment, just not fixed housing) rather than the chronically unsheltered street population, safe parking shows a comparatively high self-resolution rate — but the program remains chronically small and underfunded relative to the scale of vehicular homelessness, and is subject to yearly budget-cut threats.

**Verdict:** Worked, at small scale — comparatively strong outcomes (45% self-resolve or move to housing) for a population it's well-matched to, but capacity covers only a small fraction of LA's vehicle-dwelling homeless population and is politically vulnerable to budget cuts.

## A Bridge Home (Mayor Garcetti, launched April 2018)

**What it was:** The City of LA's pre-Inside Safe strategy for adding interim shelter beds, aiming to site at least one "bridge housing" shelter in each of the city's 15 council districts on city-owned/leased land, intended as a short-term bridge to permanent supportive housing (HHH units) coming online.

**Scale:** At its peak the program provided roughly 1,781 beds across its sites ([search-aggregated program totals](https://www.uscannenbergmedia.com/2020/02/28/a-bridge-home-project-breaks-ground-in-los-feliz/)).

**Outcomes/audit findings:** The LA City Controller's "Pathways to Permanent Housing" audit (examining city-funded interim housing, including A Bridge Home, 2019-2023) found fewer than one in five people in interim housing — a category dominated in its early years by A Bridge Home — moved on to permanent housing, calling the rate "woefully inadequate," while more than half of people leaving interim housing returned to homelessness or an unknown destination. A companion finding: roughly one in four shelter beds citywide went unused, at a cost to taxpayers estimated at $218 million ([KTLA, "L.A. audit finds very few homeless transition from temporary to permanent housing"](https://ktla.com/news/local-news/audit-finds-1-in-4-shelter-beds-in-los-angeles-went-unused-costing-taxpayers-218-million/); [LAist, "A Bridge To Where? Few Exit LA's 'Temporary' Homeless Shelters For Permanent Housing"](https://laist.com/news/bridge-home-los-angeles-garcetti-result)).

**What changed under Inside Safe:** Inside Safe kept the basic "interim housing as a bridge" logic but swapped purpose-built shelters for leased motel/hotel rooms, allowing faster, encampment-by-encampment deployment without the multi-year siting and construction process A Bridge Home required. The core structural problem the Controller identified — that interim beds don't reliably lead to permanent housing — has persisted into the Inside Safe era, based on the same audit's scope and by Inside Safe's own reported ~40% return-to-homelessness rate (see above).

**Why it worked/didn't:** A Bridge Home succeeded at the narrow goal of adding shelter capacity relatively quickly, but the audit shows the city never solved the harder problem — moving people from interim beds into permanent housing at meaningful volume — which is the same bottleneck that limits Inside Safe today.

**Verdict:** Mostly didn't work relative to its implicit goal — added real shelter capacity but functioned more as a holding pattern than a bridge, with fewer than 20% of interim-housing residents citywide (across ABH and successor programs) reaching permanent housing per the Controller's audit.

## LA Alliance for Human Rights v. City of Los Angeles

**What it is:** A federal lawsuit filed in 2020 by a coalition of downtown LA businesses and residents (LA Alliance for Human Rights) against the City and County, alleging failure to address the homelessness crisis and unsafe conditions from unmanaged encampments. It became the primary legal lever forcing city/county shelter-bed commitments.

**Key settlement terms (approved by the court June 14, 2022; 5-year term):**
- The City committed to fund/offer 6,700 new shelter beds by June 2025, and a cumulative 12,915 new shelter beds by June 2027 ([search-aggregated settlement summaries](https://cd9.lacity.gov/articles/la-alliance-human-rights-settlement)).
- The City committed to creating enough shelter/housing capacity to accommodate 60% of unsheltered people within city limits — potentially requiring 14,000-16,000 beds depending on point-in-time counts ([CNN, April 2022, settlement announcement](https://edition.cnn.com/2022/04/01/us/los-angeles-homeless-population-settlement)).
- The County separately settled in 2022-2023, committing $293 million-plus in new funding for 6,700 beds/units targeting people near freeways and unhoused seniors, plus 3,000 new mental health/substance-use treatment beds and 450 subsidies for board-and-care facilities by 2026 ([LA County, "Historic LA County and LA Alliance Settlement"](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/historic-la-county-and-la-alliance-settlement/)).
- The court retained jurisdiction to enforce the agreement, originally through 2027 (later extended to June 30, 2027 in subsequent orders).

**Compliance disputes (2024-2026):** LA Alliance filed a Motion for Settlement Compliance and Sanctions in February 2024, alleging the City had failed to deliver a plan for the 12,915 beds and missed housing milestones, and was reporting inaccurate data ("flouting" its reporting duty, per the court's later findings). In June 2025, Judge David O. Carter found the City in breach of key settlement terms but declined to place the City's roughly $1 billion in homelessness spending into court receivership, instead ordering appointment of an independent monitor ([Planning Report, "Judge Carter Rules Against Receivership," July 2025](https://www.planningreport.com/2025/07/03/la-alliance-human-rights-v-city-la-judge-carter-rules-against-receivership)). The City subsequently accused Carter of reciting the Alliance's own motion nearly verbatim in his findings ([Daily Journal, "LA misled opponents in homeless case, federal judge says"](https://www.dailyjournal.com/articles/377555-la-misled-opponents-in-homeless-case-federal-judge-says)). An evidentiary contempt hearing began in November-December 2025; on January 12, 2026, Judge Carter stayed the contempt matter, and the City filed its own motion to enforce a settlement term on December 19, 2025 (pending as of the most recent filings located) ([search-aggregated docket summaries citing cacd.uscourts.gov filings](https://www.cacd.uscourts.gov/dkt-863-la-alliance-motion-order-re-city-settlement-compliance)).

**Effect on resource allocation:** Because the settlement's core metric is shelter *beds offered* (interim capacity), critics and the court's own independent audit findings suggest it has incentivized the city to prioritize fast, cheap interim placements (motel vouchers, tiny homes) that count toward the bed target over slower permanent-housing investment — a dynamic consistent with Inside Safe's interim-heavy model and the persistently low interim-to-permanent conversion rates documented by the City Controller.

**Why it worked/didn't:** The settlement has functioned as an effective forcing mechanism to keep bed-creation on the political and budgetary agenda and has produced a court-ordered independent monitor and mandated data reporting — a meaningful accountability gain given how opaque the city's own homelessness data has been. But its "beds offered" framing does not require conversion to permanent housing, and the City has repeatedly missed its own committed milestones, triggering ongoing contempt litigation years after the original 2022 settlement.

**Verdict:** Mixed — it has kept sustained legal and political pressure on the City/County and forced independent oversight, but has not, on its own, closed the gap between interim placements and durable housing outcomes, and compliance disputes remain unresolved as of early 2026.

## CARE / CARE+ encampment cleanups

**What it is:** The City of LA's standing encampment-cleaning apparatus, distinct from and running in parallel to Inside Safe. Comprehensive Cleaning and Rapid Engagement (CARE) teams do routine, block-by-block trash/debris/hazard removal; CARE+ teams do more comprehensive cleanups that typically require encampment residents to temporarily relocate belongings. Launched October 1, 2019, under Mayor Garcetti as a rebrand explicitly intended to be less punitive and more service-connected than prior "sweep" practices ([search-aggregated program history](https://www.audacy.com/knxnews/articles/cns-news/garcetti-unveils-new-cleaning-and-rapid-engagement-or-care-teams-help-homeless)).

**Budget:** The City budgeted $56 million for CARE+ in FY2024-25, dropping to $35 million in FY2025-26 ([search-aggregated budget figures](https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/la-homeless-encampment-cleanup-program-ucla-study/811816/)).

**Effectiveness/criticism — UCLA study (January 2026):** UCLA researchers found that for every one person connected to social services through the outreach component of LA's encampment program, five people were displaced, concluding the program functions primarily as encampment clearance "with little to no outreach for shelter or housing." Among 51 unhoused residents surveyed, 37% reported being forced to relocate more than five times in the prior 30 days ([Smart Cities Dive, summarizing UCLA study, Jan. 2026](https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/la-homeless-encampment-cleanup-program-ucla-study/811816/)).

**Legal constraints — Mitchell v. Los Angeles:** A 2016 federal lawsuit (Mitchell v. Los Angeles) alleging the City's sanitation and police departments unconstitutionally seized and destroyed unhoused residents' property (IDs, medicine, belongings) resulted in a settlement requiring the City not to seize property absent an objectively reasonable belief it is abandoned, a health/safety threat, evidence of a crime, or contraband; 24-hour advance notice is required for Skid Row cleanups, and lost medicine/ID must be returned on request ([LAist, "Is LA Really Going To Federal Court To Argue It Can Seize And Destroy Homeless People's Property?"](https://laist.com/news/skid-row-mitchell-lawsuit-cleaning-downtown); [LA Downtown News, settlement coverage](https://www.ladowntownnews.com/news/legal-challenge-to-mitchell-settlement-blocked/article_6418b3e6-cb3c-11e9-bf5b-f3cc14084aa0.html)). Advocacy groups including Ktown for All continue to allege belongings are routinely destroyed in practice despite these constraints.

**Illustrative case — Echo Park Lake (March 2021):** Not formally a CARE/CARE+ operation but the highest-profile LA encampment clearance, useful as a case study in the "cleared, then what?" question. City officials pledged all ~200 residents would receive "stable, permanent housing" within a year; the clearance itself involved a large police operation, over 180 arrests, and removal of 35 tons of solid waste. A March 2022 UC Irvine-affiliated report found that of the 183 people removed, only 17 were in long-term housing a year later ([UCI News, March 2022](https://news.uci.edu/2022/03/23/of-the-183-people-removed-from-encampments-in-echo-park-lake-only-17-are-in-long-term-housing-report-says); [Urban Institute, "The Antidisplacement Fight in Echo Park Lake"](https://housingmatters.urban.org/research-summary/antidisplacement-fight-echo-park-lake-los-angeles)).

**Post-Grants Pass shift:** The Supreme Court's June 2024 Grants Pass v. Johnson decision (6-3) held that enforcing camping bans against people with nowhere else to sleep does not violate the Eighth Amendment, giving cities substantially more legal latitude to clear and cite encampments. Multiple California cities including LA "significantly stepped up" clearance efforts afterward, even as LA County's Board of Supervisors voted to decline fining or jailing people for sitting/sleeping/lying in public and affirmed people arrested at encampments would not be held in county jail — producing a split between city-level enforcement escalation and county-level resistance ([CalMatters, "Supreme Court gives cities... more power to crack down on homeless camps," June 2024](https://calmatters.org/housing/2024/06/california-homeless-camps-grants-pass-ruling/); [Governing, "California's Push to Clear Homeless Encampments"](https://www.governing.com/urban/californias-push-to-clear-homeless-encampments)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** CARE/CARE+ succeeds at its literal stated task (removing waste/hazard material from public rights-of-way) but the UCLA data indicates it is not, in practice, primarily functioning as a housing-connection tool — the 5:1 displacement-to-service ratio suggests it mainly relocates the problem rather than resolving it, consistent with RAND's LA LEADS findings on Inside Safe above.

**Verdict:** Didn't work as a homelessness-reduction tool, though it does what it is nominally chartered to do (sanitation/hazard removal) — UCLA's 2026 study and the Echo Park Lake case both show clearance heavily outpacing actual housing placement.

## Pathway Home (LA County, launched August 2023)

**What it is:** LA County's version of the encampment-resolution model, explicitly built on lessons from Project Roomkey and the City's Inside Safe program, extended to unincorporated county areas and freeway-adjacent encampments (e.g., along the I-405 and I-105) that fall outside city jurisdiction ([LA County, program launch, Aug. 2023](https://lacounty.gov/2023/08/16/los-angeles-county-successfully-launches-new-pathway-home-program-for-encampments/); [LA County, I-105 operation, April 2024](https://lacounty.gov/2024/04/18/la-countys-pathway-home-operation-near-inglewood-and-westchester-brings-nearly-600-unsheltered-people-along-the-i-105-freeway/)).

**Scale/outcomes:** As of an October 2024 comparison, Pathway Home had moved 145 people into permanent housing, versus 741 for Inside Safe over a comparable period — a smaller program by design, since it targets specific freeway-adjacent and RV-encampment clusters rather than city-wide operations ([CalMatters, Oct. 2024, cited above](https://calmatters.org/housing/homelessness/2024/10/inside-safe/)).

**Why it worked/didn't:** As a jurisdictional complement to Inside Safe (covering county land the City program can't reach), Pathway Home extends the same motel-voucher, encampment-by-encampment model to previously unaddressed freeway encampments — a genuine coverage gain — but shares Inside Safe's core interim-to-permanent bottleneck at a smaller scale.

**Verdict:** Mixed — fills a real jurisdictional gap and has moved several hundred people to permanent housing from previously unaddressed sites, but is small relative to county-wide need and has not been independently audited the way City programs have.

## LAHSA governance and the city/county funding split

**What it is:** The Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority (LAHSA), a joint city-county powers authority created in 1993, has historically administered most city and county homelessness dollars and coordinated the annual Point-in-Time count. It is not itself a program, but its governance failures materially affect every program above, since most rely on LAHSA for outreach, contracting, or data.

**2025 audit findings:** An independent review commissioned by federal Judge Carter as part of the LA Alliance litigation found LAHSA's contracting oversight was compromised — the same team approving invoices was also responsible for monitoring vendor performance — and that LAHSA routinely paid contractors without verifying services were delivered. Auditors found "significant deficiencies" in LAHSA's internal financial controls, with roughly $2.3 billion in funding whose actual expenditure and service delivery the City, County, and LAHSA could not adequately account for ([California City News, "Audit Slams LAHSA's Failures and Lack of Oversight," March 2025](https://www.californiacitynews.org/2025/03/audit-slams-lahsas-failures-and-lack-of-oversight.html); [LAist, "Searing audit finds city of LA has failed to properly track billions in homelessness spending"](https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/audit-homeless-carter-lahsa)).

**Consequence:** In response, LA County's Board of Supervisors voted to withdraw the County's roughly $300 million-plus in annual LAHSA funding, moving to administer those services directly starting July 1, 2025, effectively beginning a breakup of the joint city-county homelessness bureaucracy ([search-aggregated coverage of the County's decision](https://www.aol.com/news/l-homeless-agency-posted-solid-222205689.html)).

**Why it matters across programs:** Because Inside Safe, Pathway Home, CARE+, HHH-funded projects, and the LA Alliance settlement's own compliance reporting all depend substantially on LAHSA-supplied data and contracting, the audit's findings undercut confidence in the outcome figures reported for essentially every program in this document — a caveat that should be read into every "outcomes" section above.

**Verdict:** Didn't work as designed — the audit found systemic financial-control failures serious enough that the County chose to exit the joint agency rather than reform it, directly undermining data reliability across the entire LA homelessness-response system.

## RAND LA LEADS: independent research on encampment policy effects

**What it is:** Not a program but an ongoing independent research effort — the Los Angeles Longitudinal Enumeration and Demographic Survey (LA LEADS), run by RAND, tracking unsheltered homelessness in three LA neighborhoods (Hollywood, Venice, Skid Row) from 2021 onward. It is one of the few sources triangulating program effects (sweeps, camping bans, Inside Safe operations) against ground-level survey and enumeration data rather than city-reported program metrics.

**Key finding:** A 2024 RAND analysis found that encampment sweeps and camping-ban enforcement temporarily reduced visible homelessness, but unsheltered populations rose again within months in two of three neighborhoods studied and doubled in the third ([search-aggregated summary of 2024 RAND findings](https://news3lv.com/news/local/rand-research-finds-la-homeless-encampment-removals-not-driving-people-to-shelters)). The May 2026 update found tent dwelling down roughly 50% since 2021 (including a 23% drop in 2025 alone) but rough sleeping at a four-year high and vehicle dwelling up 11% in 2025, suggesting displacement into less visible/less stable forms of homelessness rather than net reduction — with nearly half of surveyed rough sleepers reporting a dwelling loss in the past year, 46% of those to confiscation/towing by officials ([RAND, May 2026](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2026/05/homelessness-holds-steady-across-three-la-neighborhoods.html); [SMDP coverage](https://www.smdp.com/rough-sleeping-hits-4-year-high-in-l-a-despite-flat-overall-homeless-numbers-rand-finds/)).

**Why this matters:** RAND's independent, survey-based methodology is a useful cross-check against the more optimistic program-reported figures (e.g., city tallies of "encampments resolved") cited by Inside Safe and CARE+ — and it directly supports the UCLA CARE+ displacement finding and the Echo Park Lake case above, suggesting a consistent citywide pattern: encampment-clearance-first strategies move people, not necessarily into housing.

**Verdict:** N/A (research effort, not a program) — but its findings are the strongest independent evidence available that LA's dominant clearance-oriented strategies (Inside Safe, CARE+, camping-ban enforcement) reduce visible tent encampments without proportionally reducing underlying unsheltered homelessness.

## Just In Reach (LA County, launched 2017) — jail-to-housing diversion

**What it is:** A "Pay for Success" (social-impact-bond) program that identifies people cycling through LA County jails who have chronic homelessness and behavioral health needs, and places them directly into permanent supportive housing with intensive case management, rather than releasing them back to the street.

**Scale:** Targeted 300 individuals in its initial four-year phase, funded through the region's first Pay for Success financing structure, in partnership between the County, the Conrad N. Hilton Foundation, and other funders ([Hilton Foundation, program launch](https://www.hiltonfoundation.org/news/los-angeles-county-launches-just-in-reach-supportive-housing-program-to-break-cycles-of-homelessness-and-re-incarceration/); [Third Sector Capital Partners](https://www.thirdsectorcap.org/just-in-reach-la/)).

**Outcomes:** RAND's independent evaluation found an 82% one-year housing stability rate among participants and that reduced use of county crisis services, emergency shelter, inpatient hospital stays, and jail time offset 50-100% of the program's housing costs, effectively making it close to self-funding through avoided downstream costs ([RAND research brief, "A Los Angeles Supportive Housing Program Interrupted the Cycle Between Jail and Homelessness—and Largely Paid for Itself"](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RBA1758-1.html); [RAND press release, Aug. 2022](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2022/08/16.html); [peer-reviewed cost analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10187556/)). A separate estimate found savings of about $6,200 per person in avoided county costs ([Corrections1, summarizing the study](https://www.corrections1.com/finance-and-budgets/articles/jail-diversion-program-for-la-county-homeless-saved-6200-per-person-study-finds-2Dua7PFprIbtiuIC/)).

**Why it worked:** Unlike interim-housing-first programs, Just In Reach places people directly into permanent supportive housing (skipping the interim-to-permanent bottleneck that undermines HHH, A Bridge Home, and Inside Safe) and targets a narrower, higher-need population (justice-involved, chronically homeless, behavioral-health needs) where the cost offsets from reduced jail/hospital/shelter use are large and measurable. Its small, targeted scale (300 people) made rigorous outcome tracking and RAND evaluation feasible in a way that citywide programs have not managed.

**Verdict:** Worked — one of the only LA homelessness interventions in this set with an independent, credible outcome evaluation (RAND) showing high housing retention and a program that came close to paying for itself through avoided public costs, though its scale (a few hundred people) is small relative to the countywide homeless population.

## West LA VA campus veteran housing

**What it is:** A long-running federal effort to build permanent supportive housing directly on the West Los Angeles VA Medical Center campus for homeless and at-risk veterans, following a 2015 legal settlement (Vietnam Veterans of America v. McDonald) that found the VA had misused campus land for non-veteran purposes, and a 2022 lease with the "West LA Veterans Collective" development team.

**Scale:** As of the most recent building opening (September 2024, 74 units at 671 MacArthur Ave.), 307 veteran housing units were open on campus, with construction underway on seven more buildings totaling 461 additional units. The master lease commits to a minimum of 900 units total, and PACT Act funding is intended to support at least 1,200 supportive units by 2030 ([VA.gov news release, Sept. 2024](https://www.va.gov/greater-los-angeles-health-care/news-releases/va-opens-new-building-on-west-la-campus-providing-74-additional-units-of-housing-for-homeless-and-at/)).

**Outcomes:** VA reported that in FY2024, it provided 1,647 homeless veterans in LA with permanent housing — the most of any U.S. city for the third consecutive year — alongside a 22.9% reduction in veteran homelessness in LA County between 2023 and 2024 per LAHSA's Point-in-Time count ([VA.gov, aggregated program data](https://www.va.gov/greater-los-angeles-health-care/news-releases/va-opens-two-buildings-to-provide-120-units-of-housing-for-homeless-and-at-risk-veterans-on-west-la/)).

**Why it worked:** Veterans are a population with a dedicated, better-funded, single-payer benefit system (VA housing vouchers, healthcare, and now a huge parcel of campus land specifically earmarked by court order for their housing) — a funding and land-access advantage no other subpopulation in this document has, which plausibly explains why veteran homelessness has fallen faster and further than the general unsheltered population even as citywide numbers rose in the 2026 count.

**Verdict:** Worked — the clearest population-specific success story in LA homelessness policy, driven by dedicated federal funding, a court-ordered land commitment, and a single coordinating agency (VA) rather than the fragmented city/county/nonprofit structure that hampers most other programs here.

## Measure H / Measure A (LA County homelessness sales tax)

**What it is:** Measure H, a quarter-cent county sales tax for homeless services approved by voters in 2017, was replaced in November 2024 by Measure A, a half-cent sales tax expected to generate roughly $1 billion annually, which took effect April 1, 2025. Measure A funds many of the programs above (interim housing, permanent supportive housing subsidies, prevention) rather than being a program in itself.

**Scale of funding:** LA County's Board of Supervisors approved a homeless funding plan combining $656 million from Measure A, $209 million in unspent Measure H funds, and $42.6 million in state grants for a single fiscal year ([search-aggregated Measure A spending-plan coverage](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/fiscal-year-2026-27-measure-a-spending-plan/)).

**Reported outcomes:** LA County's own "Year-One Report Card" for Measure H claimed nearly 7,500 people permanently housed in its first year, alongside thousands more in interim housing and services ([homeless.lacounty.gov, Measure H Year-One Report Card](https://homeless.lacounty.gov/news/measure-h-year-one-report-card/)) — though, per the LAHSA/LAHSA-audit caveats above, independent verification of county self-reported placement figures has been a recurring problem across this whole system.

**Why it matters here:** Measure A/H is the county-level funding backbone underneath Pathway Home, county-administered interim housing, and (following the county's 2025 withdrawal from joint LAHSA funding) an increasing share of county-run homeless services — making its own accounting integrity a load-bearing issue for every county program above.

**Verdict:** Mixed/unclear — provides large, dedicated, voter-approved recurring funding (a real structural advantage other cities lack), but self-reported outcome claims from the same county apparatus that LAHSA's 2025 audit found could not reliably account for $2.3 billion in spending should be treated with caution pending independent verification.
